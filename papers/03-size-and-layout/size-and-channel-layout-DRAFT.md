# Size and Channel Layout in Untrained Coupled Oscillator Networks for Spoken-Digit Recognition

## Abstract

<!-- DRAFT ABSTRACT, written by the agent from the design for the author to rewrite. It states the design only: no experiment has been run. -->

*Draft abstract, to be rewritten by the author.* Networks of coupled oscillators are studied as a substrate for machine learning, yet the ablations that would show how much their size and arrangement matter have not been run: no published system varies its number of oscillators, its parameter count or its channel layout against controls of the same size. We run them for untrained coupled oscillator networks read by a linear readout on speaker-disjoint spoken-digit recognition (AudioMNIST) at a signal-to-noise ratio of 0 dB, extending a previous study of one 1,024-oscillator network. The lattice ranges from 8 × 8 to 128 × 128 oscillators and the number of channels, independent parallel copies of the lattice, from 1 to 16, so the network ranges from 64 to 262,144 oscillators and from 128 to 524,288 parameters; each lattice is driven either by its own number of mel bands, one per row, or by 16 bands mapped onto its rows, and at 64 and 128 bands under two analysis windows. At every size the network is compared with an uncoupled copy of itself, a bank of leaky integrators with its states and parameters, the same readout fitted to the input alone, and five trained networks of its parameter count, and the readout is held at the same 192 features, so that any gain with size belongs to the network rather than to its reader. Besides recognizing a digit, the networks are asked which of two digits came first and to name each digit of a spoken sequence of two to four, and every network records how synchronized its oscillators are and how closely they lock to their input. The size study is crossed with six coupling functions, eight lattice geometries (a coil and a cochlea among them) and two input pathways, in nine experiments of 13,316 runs. Every coupling kernel is scaled exactly to a fixed spectral ceiling, so that the strongest coupling gain is the same at every size, and networks too large to read in memory are read channel by channel, with the same result. The design separates the effect of how many oscillators a network has from how they are arranged, and from how finely its input is resolved.

## 1 Introduction

Coupled oscillators are being revisited as a substrate for machine learning, and a previous study asked what their dynamics contribute when nothing about them is trained ([Kryski 2026b](https://github.com/ekryski/oscillator-research/blob/main/papers/02-untrained-reservoirs/)). It read one untrained coupled oscillator network, 1,024 oscillators in four channels of a 16 × 16 lattice with 2,048 parameters, by a linear readout on speaker-disjoint spoken-digit recognition, against the same readout applied to the input alone, the same network uncoupled, banks of leaky integrators of the same size and five trained networks of the same parameter count. It varied the coupling function, the lattice geometry and the way the audio enters the network, and held the network's size fixed.

Size is where that study stops, and where the literature is thinnest. A survey of oscillator networks in machine learning ([Kryski 2026a](https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.7445198)) found that geometry and capacity, though separable in principle, had been varied together by one system and "none has varied either alone", and that no published scaling study of trained oscillator networks exists along parameters or oscillator count against a conventional reference; untrained networks have not been scaled that way either. Reservoir computing treats the size of the reservoir as a primary parameter ([Lukoševičius 2012](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-642-35289-8_36)), and on spoken digits a reservoir's accuracy has been seen to grow with it: a liquid of spiking neurons, shaped by unsupervised plasticity rather than left untrained, recognized TI-46 digits at 77.7% with 800 neurons and 86.66% with 3,200, and an ensemble of two liquids of 1,600 neurons reached 85.14% with 3.45 times fewer training examples and 1.95 times sparser connectivity ([Srinivasan et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnins.2018.00524)). An oscillator network can likewise grow in two ways: each of its lattices can be made larger, or more lattices can be added beside the first. The two give the same number of oscillators in different arrangements, and whether the arrangement matters has not been asked of oscillator networks.

This paper asks it, for the previous study's networks and on its task, protocol and readout, so that its 16 × 16, four-channel runs are that study's own. It asks questions rather than testing hypotheses. How does the network's task accuracy change with its number of oscillators, from 64 to 262,144, when the readout does not grow with it? Does its margin over an uncoupled copy of itself, over a leaky-integrator bank of the same size and over its input alone change with size? At a fixed number of oscillators, does it matter whether they form a few large lattices or many small ones? Does it matter whether each lattice row is driven by a mel band of its own or shares one with its neighbours, and, where the bands are narrowest, how long a stretch of audio the front end analyses? How do trained networks of the same parameter count compare as both grow, and are untrained oscillators more useful than trained networks at the smallest sizes? Does the network's memory, for which of two digits came first and for each digit of a spoken sequence, change with its size? Do the previous study's effects of coupling function, lattice geometry (a coil and a cochlea among them) and input pathway depend on size? And how do the network's synchronization and its locking to its input change with its size and layout?

## 2 Background

Coupled oscillators synchronize, form clusters, lock to a drive or split into coherent and incoherent domains, depending on how they are coupled and arranged ([Pikovsky et al. 2001](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511755743); [Strogatz 2000](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0167-2789%2800%2900094-4); [Kuramoto & Battogtokh 2002](https://arxiv.org/abs/cond-mat/0210694)). Untrained and read by a trained linear readout, they are reservoirs in the sense of reservoir computing ([Jaeger 2001](https://www.ai.rug.nl/minds/uploads/EchoStatesTechRep.pdf); [Maass et al. 2002](https://doi.org/10.1162/089976602760407955); [Tanaka et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neunet.2019.03.005)), where the reservoir's size is a primary design parameter ([Lukoševičius 2012](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-642-35289-8_36)). The previous study ([Kryski 2026b](https://github.com/ekryski/oscillator-research/blob/main/papers/02-untrained-reservoirs/)) gives the physics under test and its place in machine learning in full; this section restates what the size study needs.

The network here is C channels of a G × G lattice. Each channel is an independent copy: it receives the same input, has its own coupling kernel and natural frequencies, and does not act on any other channel, so channels are side by side like the heads of one attention layer, not stacked like layers (Appendix C). Within a channel every oscillator is coupled to every other through the kernel, and the lattice's rows follow frequency, row r driven by the r-th input band. A network therefore grows either by enlarging each channel's lattice, which adds coupled neighbours and input rows, or by adding channels, which adds independent copies driven alike.

The six coupling functions and eight lattice geometries are the previous study's (Appendix F). A coupling function sets how the phases of two coupled oscillators turn into a push on one of them, from the Kuramoto function that pulls each oscillator toward the others' phases to the Stuart–Landau function, whose oscillators have an amplitude as well as a phase; the survey found no study that compares them ([Kryski 2026a](https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.7445198)). A lattice geometry changes only which oscillators are neighbours, how the grid's edges are glued, from the torus to a coil and a cochlea that follow the shape and mechanics of the inner ear; the survey found geometry varied in four machine-learning systems, "and in none of them as a single variable". In both, the kernel, the parameters and the oscillators are the same, so each comparison varies one thing at every size.

## 3 Methods

### 3.1 Design

This study asks how an untrained coupled oscillator network's task accuracy changes with its number of oscillators when the readout does not grow with it; whether its margins over an uncoupled copy of itself, over a leaky-integrator bank of the same size and over its input alone change with size; whether the layout of a fixed number of oscillators matters; whether the band mapping and, at 64 and 128 bands, the analysis window matter; how trained networks of the same parameter count compare, at the smallest sizes in particular; how the network's memory for order and for sequences changes with size; whether the effects of coupling function, lattice geometry and input pathway depend on size; and how its synchronization and locking to the input change with size, layout, coupling function and geometry. It runs as nine experiments of 13,316 runs in total (Appendix B). Every run is one arm at one lattice size, channel count, band mapping, analysis window, input pathway, task and seed, at a signal-to-noise ratio of 0 dB and input gain 1; each condition is run at three seeds. 234 of the runs are the previous study's own, at 16 × 16 with four channels (Section 3.5). Appendix A defines every term and Appendix G gives the methods in full.

Every oscillator network is C channels of a G × G lattice, with G from 8 to 128 and C from 1 to 16: C · G² oscillators, from 64 to 262,144, and 2 · C · G² parameters, a kernel weight and a natural frequency per oscillator, from 128 to 524,288 (Figure 1). Each oscillator's phase evolves as θ̇ᵢ = ωᵢ + couplingᵢ + g·uᵢ − λ sin θᵢ: its natural frequency, the coupling from the other oscillators of its channel through a random kernel, the input at gain g, and a restoring pull of strength λ. The reference configuration is the previous study's: Kuramoto coupling on the torus with random natural frequencies, restoring strength 0.3 and coupling ceiling 1. The previous study only lowered a channel's kernel to the _coupling ceiling_ when the peak of its spectrum exceeded it; here every kernel is scaled so that its peak equals the ceiling exactly, which is the same rule at 16 × 16, where a random kernel's peak always exceeds it, but raises the kernels of the smallest lattices, whose peaks usually fall short (Appendix G.4). Doubling G and quartering C leaves the number of oscillators unchanged, so nine counts are each reached by two or three layouts. Around the reference, six coupling functions and eight lattice geometries, a coil and a cochlea among them, are varied at every size (Appendix F).

A lattice of G rows is driven either by G mel bands, one per row, or by the previous study's 16 bands mapped onto its rows: the first gives a larger lattice a finer input, the second holds the input fixed and varies only the network. At 64 and 128 bands the lattices driven by their own bands are also run under a longer analysis window, 1,024 or 2,048 samples, which resolves the narrow low bands the 512-sample window cannot, with the frames unchanged (Appendix G.3).

The arms compared with it are matched to its size: the **spectrogram-only baseline**, the readout applied directly to the rows the network is driven by; the **uncoupled oscillator network**, the same network with its coupling kernels set to zero; the **state-matched leaky-integrator bank**, C · G² untrained leaky integrators with the network's states and parameters (the previous study's width-matched bank is the state-matched bank of twice the channels); and five **trained baselines** (GRU, a dilated residual TCN, CNN, transformer and S4D), each with one width knob set so its parameter count meets the network's, trained end to end with the previous study's recipe on the same rows (Appendix G.5). The three untrained dynamical arms are the reservoirs.

### 3.2 Task, data and noise

The data are the previous study's: the 30,000 recordings of AudioMNIST ([Becker et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jfranklin.2023.11.038)), ten spoken digits by 60 speakers. Every result keeps speakers apart under its Protocol A: the arms train on 2,048 clips of speakers 1 to 48, a different draw for each seed, and are tested on recordings of speakers 49 to 60. White noise is added to every clip at a signal-to-noise ratio of 0 dB, as loud as the speech, the lower of the previous study's two noise levels, where the task is far from saturating. There are three tasks. Recognition names the digit of one clip and is tested on all 6,000 recordings of the test speakers; chance is 10%. The previous study's order task asks which of two digits of a pair came first, on five pairs; read over the whole span, the input alone reads chance, 50%, so anything above it comes from an arm's own memory. The digit-sequence task, new here, asks for the digit at each position of a sequence of two, three or four different digits, each position by a readout of its own; chance at each position is 10%, and a reader that knows which digits a sequence holds but not their order, as the input read over the whole span nearly does, reaches 1/L at each position of a sequence of L. Every arm hears the previous study's fixed front end, a log-mel spectrogram at 62.5 frames per second with nothing learned; from 64 bands its transform is zero-padded so that no mel filter falls between its frequency bins, with the frames unchanged (Appendix G.3).

### 3.3 Readout

Every arm is read the previous study's way. Each of its signals (a band energy, the sine or cosine of an oscillator's phase, a leaky integrator's state or a trained baseline's hidden unit) is summarized by its mean, standard deviation and mean absolute change over each of four equal windows of frames 16 to 61, after a 16-frame warm-up (the whole span from frame 16 as one window for the memory tasks). So that readout capacity cannot pass for dynamics, every arm is read at the same width, 192 standardized features: an arm with more features is projected down to 192 by a random Gaussian matrix ([Johnson & Lindenstrauss 1984](https://doi.org/10.1090/conm/026/737400)) and one with fewer is read as it is. The readout's 1,930 weights are therefore the same at every size: the network grows and its reader does not, so a gain with size belongs to the network. Widths of 1,024 and 4,096 show what the projection costs a larger network. The features are classified by one linear layer fitted by ridge regression in closed form ([Jaeger 2001](https://www.ai.rug.nl/minds/uploads/EchoStatesTechRep.pdf); [Lukoševičius 2012](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-642-35289-8_36)), one per position on the digit-sequence task. Every projected read is recorded under the previous study's fixed projection and under one drawn from each run's seed, so that the spread over seeds can include the projection's own draw. A network of more than 4,096 states (from 32 × 32 at 8 channels) does not fit in memory and is read channel by channel, which gives the in-memory read's accuracies exactly (Appendix G.6). Every coupled and uncoupled network is also read with its rotation rates added, the previous study's secondary read, and records how synchronized its oscillators are and how closely they lock to their own band's drive: the order parameter R, each oscillator's phase-locking value and the share of oscillators locked (Appendix G.7). Nothing reads these instruments; they are reported beside the accuracies.

### 3.4 Input pathways and gain

The audio drives a reservoir in one of the previous study's two ways: the **spectrogram** pathway adds each band's energy to the rotation rate of its row's oscillators, and the **quadrature** pathway adds a phase-referenced push g·Aᵣ sin(φᵣ − θᵢ), the injection form of [Adler 1946](https://doi.org/10.1109/JRPROC.1946.229930), so an oscillator can lock to its band's own cycle; the leaky-integrator bank has no phase for it to act on. Every reservoir runs at input gain 1, where the band energies push with about the magnitude of the natural frequencies. The coupling adds at most 0.1 radian a step at every lattice size, because the ceiling holds the kernel's largest gain at 1, and the input about 0.15 at gain 1, far below the integrator's bound of π a step ([Hairer et al. 1993](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-540-78862-1)). The spectrogram-only baseline and the trained baselines take no gain (Appendix G.10).

### 3.5 Reporting

Every task accuracy reported is the mean over three seeds ± the sample standard deviation, under the fixed and under the seeded projection, and every comparison between two arms is paired on the same test clips, condition and seed, at the same lattice, band mapping, analysis window and channel count, and reported as the mean difference ± its standard deviation over seeds and a 95% interval from 2,000 paired bootstrap resamples of the test clips. The primary cell for every comparison is width 192, 2,048 training clips and the task's primary read (four windows for recognition, the whole span for the memory tasks). No threshold decides a result. Of the 234 runs the previous study made, 66 are taken from its record as it recorded them; the other 168 lack a projection or a read reported here and are run again on the CPU, and a reuse check runs a sample of them again and compares every cell with that record, as a leak check reads every reservoir with no input at every size (Appendix G.11). Runs use an NVIDIA GPU, the GPU of Apple Silicon or the CPU, and record which; only the CPU reproduces the previous study bit for bit (Appendix E).

## Appendix {-}

## A Glossary

The terms as this paper uses them, carried over from [Kryski 2026b](https://github.com/ekryski/oscillator-research/blob/main/papers/02-untrained-reservoirs/) and extended for lattice size, channels, band mapping and parameter matching. The code and the run record keep their own labels; src/README.md maps each term to its label.

### A.1 The pipeline

| Term | As used in this paper |
|---|---|
| **Front end** | The fixed first stage every arm shares. It turns a clip's 16 kHz waveform into a mel spectrogram: a Fourier transform of a 512-sample (32 ms) Hann window every 256 samples (16 ms), G mel bands, the log of each band's energy, and a fixed rescaling. At 8, 16 and 32 bands it is the previous paper's exactly. At 64 and 128 bands the transform is zero-padded to 1,024 and 2,048 points behind the same window and frames, so that no band's filter falls between the transform's frequency bins; zero-padding samples the same spectrum more finely and adds no frequency resolution, so those band counts are also run under a longer analysis window. Nothing in it is trained or fitted to the data. |
| **Analysis window** | The stretch of audio each frame of the front end transforms: 512 samples (32 ms), as in the previous paper, at every band count; at 64 and 128 bands also 1,024 (64 ms) and 2,048 (128 ms), with every frame centred where the 512-sample frame is, so the frames and the read are unchanged. Not to be confused with the read's windows (A.7). |
| **Mel band** | One of the G frequency ranges the front end splits the audio into, spaced on the mel scale, which follows pitch perception: narrow at low frequencies, wide at high ones. G is 8, 16, 32, 64 or 128. |
| **Band energy** | The log energy in one mel band, one value per frame: the signal the front end produces for that band. |
| **Mel spectrogram** | A clip's band energies together, frame by frame. The spectrogram-only baseline reads it directly; every reservoir is driven by it. |
| **Frame** | One 16 ms step of the front end, 62.5 per second, the same at every band count. |
| **Reservoir** | An untrained dynamical system between the front end and the readout: here the coupled and uncoupled oscillator networks and the leaky-integrator bank. The term is reservoir computing's ([Jaeger 2001](https://www.ai.rug.nl/minds/uploads/EchoStatesTechRep.pdf); [Maass et al. 2002](https://doi.org/10.1162/089976602760407955)). |
| **Readout** | The reader shared by every arm, and the only fitted part of an untrained arm: one linear layer from 192 features to 10 digit scores (1,930 weights), fitted by ridge regression, that is least squares with an L2 penalty, solved in closed form. The penalty is chosen from four values on the last eighth of the training clips, then the readout is refit on all of them. It is the same for every arm at every size, so it is held constant across comparisons: as a network grows, its readout does not. |
| **Untrained** | Parameters drawn at random once, from the seed, and never updated. In an untrained arm only the readout is fitted. |
| **Input gain** | The factor that scales the input before it drives a reservoir: 1 here. It applies only to the reservoirs: the spectrogram-only baseline has no dynamics for it to act on, and a trained baseline learns its own input scale. |
| **Input pathway** | How the audio drives a reservoir. Spectrogram: each band energy adds to the rotation rate of the oscillators in its row. Quadrature: each band's energy and phase, so the push depends on where an oscillator is relative to the band's own cycle, a phase-referenced drive of the kind [Adler 1946](https://doi.org/10.1109/JRPROC.1946.229930) analysed. |

### A.2 Arms and baselines

| Term | As used in this paper |
|---|---|
| **Arm** | One system under comparison, read the same way as every other. The term comes from experimental design. |
| **Spectrogram-only baseline** | The front end and the readout with nothing between them: the readout reads the band energies directly, so it measures what the input alone supports. At every lattice it reads exactly the rows that lattice's networks are driven with. Read from the first frame, over the whole clip, it is the primary control, because it sees everything a reservoir is driven with; read from frame 16, it sees the frames every other arm is read over. On the quadrature pathway it reads that pathway's front end instead. |
| **Coupled oscillator network** | The system under test: C channels of a G × G lattice, C · G² untrained oscillators coupled within each channel through a coupling kernel, with 2 · C · G² parameters (the kernels and the natural frequencies). Also "the oscillator network". The previous paper's network is 4 channels of a 16 × 16 lattice: 1,024 oscillators and 2,048 parameters. Its reference configuration, used wherever an experiment does not vary it: Kuramoto coupling, torus geometry, random natural frequencies, restoring strength 0.3, coupling ceiling 1. |
| **Uncoupled oscillator network** | The same network with every coupling kernel set to zero: each oscillator keeps its natural frequency, restoring strength and input, but none acts on another. It isolates what the coupling contributes. With no coupling term it does not depend on the coupling function or the lattice geometry. |
| **Leaky integrator** | A unit that holds one number x and, at each frame, moves a fraction a of the way toward its input: x ← (1 − a)·x + a·tanh(g_in·g·u), where u is the input, g the input gain, g_in the unit's own input weight, and tanh bounds the input. The old value decays exponentially, or "leaks", so the unit is a running average of its recent input with a time constant set by a; in signal-processing terms it is a first-order low-pass filter. In computational neuroscience it is the leaky-integrator neuron, the leaky integrate-and-fire model without the firing; in reservoir computing it is the unit of leaky-integrator echo state networks ([Jaeger et al. 2007](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neunet.2007.04.016)), whose a is the "leaking rate" ([Lukoševičius 2012](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-642-35289-8_36)). |
| **Leaky-integrator bank** | A reservoir of independent leaky integrators, routed like the oscillator network: mel band r drives every unit in row r. Time constants are spaced logarithmically from 16 ms to 1 s across the units of each row, input weights g_in are drawn from N(1, 0.1²), and no unit is connected to another: nothing rotates and nothing is coupled. State-matched: C · G² units, the oscillator network's number of states and parameters, at every size. Width-matched: twice the units, matching the two signals the network exposes per oscillator, at twice the parameters; it is the state-matched bank of 2C channels. |
| **Trained baselines** | Five conventional networks, trained end to end (AdamW, 30 epochs) through a learned linear layer on the same statistics, standardized, then read by the same readout, each sized to a network and driven by that network's rows. GRU: a gated recurrent network. TCN: a temporal convolutional network, two residual blocks of dilated causal convolutions (receptive field 31 frames). CNN: two causal one-dimensional convolutions (receptive field 9 frames). Transformer: one causal self-attention layer with two heads and a two-layer feed-forward block. S4D: a diagonal state-space model, its time constants initialized from 2 to 200 frames. |
| **Matched pair** | Two runs identical in every factor but the one compared, at the same lattice, band mapping, analysis window, channel count, noise level, input gain and seed. A factor's effect is the mean difference over its matched pairs. |
| **Seed** | Sets an arm's random parameters and which training clips it draws. Every condition is run at three seeds, and results are reported as their mean with the standard deviation. |

### A.3 Size and layout

| Term | As used in this paper |
|---|---|
| **Lattice size** | The side G of the G × G lattice a channel's oscillators sit on: 8, 16, 32, 64 or 128. |
| **Channel count** | The number C of channels in a network: 1, 2, 4, 8 or 16. |
| **Number of oscillators** | C · G², from 64 (8 × 8, one channel) to 262,144 (128 × 128, 16 channels). Also the number of states of the network and of its state-matched bank. |
| **Layout** | How a network's oscillators are divided: into few large channels or many small ones. Several layouts share a number of oscillators, because doubling G and quartering C leaves C · G² unchanged: 1,024 oscillators are 8 × 8 at 16 channels, 16 × 16 at 4 and 32 × 32 at 1. |
| **Parameter count** | The number of parameters of an arm. For the coupled oscillator network, 2 · C · G²: one kernel weight per offset and one natural frequency per oscillator, per channel. The uncoupled network stores as many and uses half; the state-matched bank has as many by construction; the spectrogram-only baseline has none. |
| **Parameter matching** | Sizing a trained baseline to a network's parameter count. Each architecture has one width knob (the GRU's hidden size, the TCN's and the CNN's hidden channels, the transformer's model width, the S4D's width and state count) and the rest of its design fixed; the width is the widest whose parameter count does not exceed the network's, except the CNN's, the narrowest that reaches it. At 2,048 parameters on 16 rows this gives the previous paper's baselines, 1,840 to 2,109 parameters; from 2,048 parameters up every baseline is within 15% of its network, and below that some are coarser, as the record shows. |

### A.4 The oscillator network

| Term | As used in this paper |
|---|---|
| **Oscillator** | A unit whose state advances around a cycle. A phase oscillator keeps only its phase θ, an angle; a Stuart–Landau oscillator also keeps an amplitude. The readout sees sin θ and cos θ of each. |
| **Natural frequency ω** | The rate at which an oscillator would advance if nothing acted on it. Random: drawn from N(1, 0.1²), the same distribution at every size. The previous paper also ran tonotopic and identical natural frequencies; this one draws them at random only. |
| **Lattice** | The G × G grid a channel's oscillators sit on. Rows follow frequency: the input to row r comes from the r-th band, from lowest to highest. |
| **Row** | The G oscillators of one channel that one input row drives. The only grouping built into the network. |
| **Band mapping** | How a front end's mel bands drive a lattice's rows. One band per row: a G × G lattice is driven by G mel bands. Mapped: the 16 bands of the previous paper drive the rows, each band G/16 adjacent rows of a larger lattice, each row of an 8 × 8 lattice the mean of two adjacent bands. At 16 × 16 the two are the same. Nothing in the mapping is fitted. |
| **Channel** | One independent G × G lattice of oscillators, with its own coupling kernel and natural frequencies. Every channel receives the same input, and channels do not act on each other: they are parallel copies, like the heads of one attention layer, read side by side, not layers stacked one on another (Appendix C). The name follows the channels of a convolutional network, where each channel likewise has its own kernel. |
| **Coupling kernel** | A channel's table of coupling weights: a G × G array whose entry at an offset of so many rows and columns sets how strongly an oscillator is acted on by the one at that offset. The same table applies at every site, so the coupling is a convolution, and "kernel" is meant as in a convolutional network, not as a GPU compute kernel. It covers every offset, so each oscillator is coupled to every other in its channel. The weights are drawn from N(0, 0.05²) and the kernel is then scaled to the coupling ceiling: a positive weight pulls a pair toward the same phase, a negative one pushes it apart. |
| **Coupling function** | How the phases of two coupled oscillators turn into a push on one of them (A.5). |
| **Coupling ceiling** | Each channel's overall coupling strength, 1: the largest factor by which its kernel amplifies any spatial pattern of phases across the channel (the peak of the kernel's spectrum). Every channel's kernel is scaled, all weights by one factor, so that its peak equals the ceiling exactly, raised or lowered as needed; the strongest coupling gain is therefore the same at every size. It does not cap individual pairs, neighbours or regions, and since a random kernel's peak grows with the lattice, the individual weights of a larger lattice end up smaller. The previous paper only lowered a kernel to the ceiling; random 16 × 16 kernels always exceed it, so on that lattice the two rules are the same. |
| **Restoring strength λ** | The strength of a pull on every oscillator back toward phase 0, the term −λ sin θ, 0.3. It has the form of the restoring torque that returns a pendulum to rest, but the oscillators have no inertia, so nothing swings back past 0: an oscillator whose natural frequency is below λ is held in place, or locked ([Adler 1946](https://doi.org/10.1109/JRPROC.1946.229930)), and a faster one keeps rotating, slowed where the pull opposes it. For Stuart–Landau oscillators it is a pull toward phase 0 at unit amplitude. |
| **Cluster** | A group of oscillators that move together, in phase or in fixed opposition, because of the dynamics rather than the wiring. Nothing in a kernel assigns an oscillator to a cluster; clusters form, or do not, as the network runs ([Pikovsky et al. 2001](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511755743)). |

### A.7 The read and the evaluation

| Term | As used in this paper |
|---|---|
| **Signal** | A time series an arm exposes to the readout: a band energy for the spectrogram-only baseline, sin θ or cos θ of an oscillator, a leaky integrator's state, or a trained baseline's hidden unit. |
| **Summary statistics** | Per signal and window: the mean, the standard deviation and the mean absolute frame-to-frame change. None is an endpoint, and the absolute change cannot tell a rising signal from a falling one. |
| **Window** | For recognition, frames 16 to 61 split into four equal windows; for the memory tasks, frames 16 to the clip's end as one. Every clip of a task is read over the same frames. |
| **Rotation rate** | How far an oscillator's phase advances between frames, read as the mean of cos Δθ and sin Δθ over a window: two more numbers per oscillator and window. A second read of every coupled and uncoupled network, beside the statistics. |
| **Warm-up** | The first 16 frames (256 ms), which every arm but the whole-clip spectrogram-only baseline skips. Reading every arm over the same frames after the warm-up means an arm with no input reads chance. |
| **Projection** | The Gaussian matrix that brings an arm's features to a common width. Fixed: the previous paper's, one draw for every seed. Seeded: drawn from the run's seed, so the spread over seeds includes the draw. Every projected read is reported under both. |
| **Width** | The number of features the readout sees: 192, 1,024 or 4,096. An arm with more features than that is projected down to it; one with fewer is read as it is (Section 3.3). An arm's native width is its feature count before projection, 24 per oscillator for the network (two signals, three statistics, four windows): 24,576 for the previous paper's network and 6,291,456 for a 128 × 128 network of 16 channels. |
| **Channel-by-channel read** | How an arm of more than 4,096 states is read, since its features do not fit in memory: one read at a time, each channel is run alone, its features standardized with its own training statistics and projected, and the projections added. Because channels do not act on each other and standardization is per feature, this is the same read as holding every feature at once, up to the order of floating-point sums; its projection is drawn channel by channel, with the same distribution. |
| **Primary cell** | Width 192, 2,048 training clips, and the four-window read (the whole-span read for the memory tasks). |
| **Protocol A** | Train on speakers 1 to 48 and test on speakers 49 to 60 of AudioMNIST ([Becker et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jfranklin.2023.11.038)): on all 6,000 of their recordings for recognition. |
| **Noise level** | White noise added to each clip at a level set by its speech, given as the signal-to-noise ratio. Every run here is at 0 dB, noise as loud as the speech. |
| **Order task** | Two digits of a pair spoken one after the other; the task is to say which came first. The mean and spread of a signal do not depend on the order of its frames, so the input alone read over the whole span cannot answer it, and the spectrogram-only baseline reads chance, 50%. |
| **Digit-sequence task** | Two, three or four different digits spoken one after the other; the task is to name the digit at each position, each position by a readout of its own. Chance at a position is 10%. |
| **Order-free level** | What a reader that knows which digits a sequence holds, but not their order, reaches: 1/L at each position of a sequence of L digits, and every position right with probability 1/L!. The input read over the whole span is close to such a reader. |
| **Leak check** | The reservoirs read with no input (input gain 0) at every lattice size, in memory and channel by channel: every cell should read exactly chance, or the read or the data would be leaking around the reservoir. |
| **Reuse check** | A sample of the previous paper's runs made again with this paper's code on the CPU, every cell compared with the previous paper's record. |

### A.8 Synchronization and locking

| Term | As used in this paper |
|---|---|
| **Order parameter R** | How synchronized a channel is: the magnitude of the mean of e^{iθ} over its oscillators, 1 when all share one phase and near 0 when their phases are spread; averaged over channels and frames. |
| **Locking to the drive** | How closely an oscillator follows its own row's input: the phase-locking value, the magnitude of the mean over frames of e^{i(θ − φ)}, where φ is the phase of the row's drive; averaged over a network's oscillators. |
| **Entrained** | An oscillator whose locking value exceeds 0.5. The share entrained is reported with R and the locking. |

## E Computation: devices and cost

Every run can use an NVIDIA GPU, the GPU of Apple Silicon or the CPU, and records which it used. The front end's rows are computed on the CPU and cached, so every device is driven by the same input; the untrained arms are built on the CPU from their seed, so every device runs the same kernels and natural frequencies, and are then simulated on the device; a trained baseline is built on the CPU and, as in the previous study, trained there unless a run is told otherwise; and the readout projects on the device and solves its ridge regression on the CPU in double precision. On each device the coupling runs through the fast Fourier transform or as the same operator written as a dense matrix, whichever measured faster there: dense up to 16 × 16 and by transform above on the CPU, dense up to 64 × 64 on the Apple GPU, and by transform on NVIDIA GPUs. The two are the same operator to rounding on every geometry.

Only the CPU reproduces the previous study bit for bit. A GPU's floating-point results are close to the CPU's but not identical, because its transforms, matrix products and reductions sum in other orders; the harness's tests hold every model, front end and trained baseline on the Apple GPU to the CPU's results within rounding. ## F Coupling functions and lattice geometries

### F.1 Coupling functions

Each oscillator's phase θᵢ evolves as θ̇ᵢ = ωᵢ + couplingᵢ + g·uᵢ − λ sin θᵢ: its natural frequency, the coupling from the other oscillators of its channel, the input and a restoring pull toward phase 0. The six coupling functions are the previous study's and differ only in the coupling term; the survey found no study that compares them ([Kryski 2026a](https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.7445198)). Appendix D draws how each pushes an oscillator.

| coupling function | coupling term | what it adds | in machine learning |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Kuramoto** | Σⱼ Kᵢⱼ sin(θⱼ − θᵢ) | pulls each oscillator toward the others' phases: the minimal model of synchronization ([Kuramoto 1975](https://doi.org/10.1007/BFb0013365)), and the reference | trained all-to-all in Un-0 ([unconv.ai 2026](https://unconv.ai/blog/introducing-un-0-generating-images-with-coupled-oscillators/)); a D-dimensional generalization in AKOrN ([Miyato et al. 2025](https://openreview.net/forum?id=nwDRD4AMoN)) |
| **Kuramoto–Sakaguchi** | Σⱼ Kᵢⱼ sin(θⱼ − θᵢ − α), α = π/4 | a phase lag that breaks the pull's symmetry and admits partial coherence ([Sakaguchi & Kuramoto 1986](https://doi.org/10.1143/PTP.76.576); [Abrams & Strogatz 2004](https://arxiv.org/abs/nlin/0407045)) | with Daido harmonics and a delay in FSN ([Nunley 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.18694)) |
| **Second harmonic** | Kuramoto plus β Σⱼ Kᵢⱼ sin 2(θⱼ − θᵢ), β = 0.5 | favours two-cluster states, pairs in phase or in opposition ([Daido 1992](https://doi.org/10.1143/ptp/88.6.1213); [Hansel et al. 1993](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevE.48.3470)) | none recorded |

| **Stuart–Landau** | Σⱼ Kᵢⱼ (zⱼ − zᵢ), z = x + iy | amplitude as well as phase: each oscillator relaxes to a cycle of unit radius ([Stuart 1960](https://doi.org/10.1017/S002211206000116X); [Aranson & Kramer 2002](https://doi.org/10.1103/RevModPhys.74.99)) | one graph network |

### F.2 Lattice geometries

Every channel stores its G² oscillators as a G × G grid whose row r is driven by the r-th input band. A lattice geometry changes only which oscillators are neighbours, that is, how the grid's edges are glued; each channel is a separate copy of the same shape, and the kernel, the parameters and the oscillators are the same under every geometry, so a comparison between geometries varies one thing at every size. The torus is the reference: wrapped in both directions, it has no edges, so every oscillator has the same neighbourhood. The first six geometries vary the number of wrapped axes (sheet, cylinder, torus), the dimension (the cube, which shortens the paths between oscillators) and how frequency is laid out, the cylinder and the sphere leaving the frequency axis open as the cochlea does and the helix putting rows a quarter of the band range apart next to each other, as the pitch helix of music perception puts octaves ([Shepard 1982](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.89.4.305)). The helix is not a cochlea: it is closed, and nothing in it runs one way. The coil and the cochlea follow the cochlea itself, whose coiled, tonotopic shape a spiral metamaterial resonator has used to classify AudioMNIST digits ([Beoletto et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1002/aisy.202500526)). Oscillator lattices are a long-studied setting in physics ([Sakaguchi et al. 1987](https://doi.org/10.1143/PTP.77.1005)); the survey found geometry varied in four machine-learning systems, "and in none of them as a single variable" ([Kryski 2026a](https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.7445198)).

| geometry | how the grid is glued |
|---|---|
| **Torus** | Both axes wrap: the top row is coupled to the bottom, and the left column to the right. The reference geometry. |
| **Cylinder** | Columns wrap, rows do not: the frequency axis is open, so the highest band is not coupled around to the lowest. |
| **Sheet** | Neither axis wraps, and coupling stops at every edge. |
| **Helix** | The G² sites read as one closed ring, G/4 rows per turn, so an offset of one turn joins rows a quarter of the band range apart. |
| **Cube** | Each row's G oscillators read as an a × b slab, giving a G × a × b lattice that wraps on all three axes. The slab is as nearly square as the row allows: 4 × 4 and 8 × 8 at 16 and 64 (the previous study's is 4 × 4), and 2 × 4, 4 × 8 and 8 × 16 at 8, 32 and 128, where G is not a square. |
| **Sphere** | Rows as latitudes with open poles and columns as longitudes that wrap, each oscillator's influence weighted by the cosine of its latitude: an approximation to a sphere, not exact spherical coupling. |
| **Coil** | The G² sites read as one open line from the base (highest band) to the apex (lowest band), G/4 rows per turn: the helix with its ends apart, as the cochlea's spiral is open at both ends, so an offset of one turn joins sites that sit side by side on adjacent turns. |
| **Cochlea** | The coil with two features of the cochlea's mechanics: influence runs three times as strongly from base to apex as back, as the travelling wave does, and the coupling into each site grows with the coil's curvature, from a quarter at the base to full strength at the apex, as the spiral tightens. A control, the cochlea at the coil's average coupling, keeps both but rescales the curvature to the coil's average coupling. |

Table: The eight lattice geometries, at a lattice of G × G. The first six run in the design experiments, the coil and the cochlea, with the cochlea's control, in the cochlea experiment (Appendix B).

The previous study ran the coil and the cochlea at 16 × 16, and everything that defines them is a share of the coil's length, so each is one geometry at every lattice size. The coil makes four turns at every size, G/4 rows and G²/4 sites to a turn: the previous study's 64 sites, four rows, at 16 × 16, and two rows at 8 × 8. A channel's G² kernel weights are its couplings at the signed offsets from −G²/2 to G²/2 − 1 along the line, so each site is coupled to the sites up to two turns away and the line never closes on itself. The cochlea's curvature weights fall from 1 at the apex to a quarter at the base over the whole line, as a logarithmic spiral's radius falls over its four turns, and the control rescales them to average 1, which puts its apex at about 1.85 times the ceiling at every size. The ceiling is met exactly as on every other geometry: the direction is applied to the kernel before its spectrum is scaled, and the curvature weights, which never exceed 1 outside the control, can only lower the coupling. The harness's tests check each of these at every lattice size from 8 × 8 to 128 × 128.

## G Methods in full

### G.1 Experimental design

The study asks eight questions:

1. How does the coupled oscillator network's task accuracy change with its number of oscillators, C · G², when the readout does not grow with it?
2. Does the network's margin over its uncoupled copy, its state-matched leaky-integrator bank and the spectrogram-only baseline on the same rows change with size, and what do its rotation rates, the previous study's secondary read, add at each size?
3. At a fixed number of oscillators, does it matter whether they form few large channels or many small ones? Nine counts, from 256 oscillators (8 × 8 at 4 channels, 16 × 16 at 1) to 65,536 (64 × 64 at 16, 128 × 128 at 4), are each reached by two or three layouts; 1,024 oscillators, for example, are 8 × 8 at 16 channels, 16 × 16 at 4 (the previous study's network) and 32 × 32 at 1.
4. At a fixed lattice, does driving each row with its own mel band read differently from mapping the previous study's 16 bands onto the rows, and at 64 and 128 bands, does a longer analysis window, which resolves the narrow low bands the previous study's window cannot, read differently from its window?
5. How do trained baselines of the network's parameter count compare as both grow? Are untrained oscillator networks more useful than trained networks of the same size at the smallest sizes, from 128 to about 2,048 parameters, and does any such advantage persist, shrink or reverse as both grow?
6. How does the network's ability to tell the order of two digits, and to name the digit at each position of a sequence of two, three or four, change with size and layout, against the same controls?
7. Do the previous study's effects of coupling function, lattice geometry and input pathway depend on size?
8. How do the network's synchronization and its locking to its drive change with lattice size and channel count, and with the coupling function and the lattice geometry?

It runs as nine experiments of 13,316 runs in all (Appendix B), two of them checks on the pipeline: the leak check and the reuse check (Appendix G.11). Every run is one arm, one of the systems compared, at one lattice size, channel count, band mapping, analysis window, input pathway, task and random seed, at a signal-to-noise ratio of 0 dB and input gain 1. The lattice is 8 × 8, 16 × 16, 32 × 32, 64 × 64 or 128 × 128, the channel count 1, 2, 4, 8 or 16, every lattice other than 16 × 16 is driven under two band mappings (Figure 1), and the lattices driven by 64 or 128 bands of their own under two analysis windows (Appendix G.3). Each arm's signals are summarized, brought to a common width and classified by the same linear readout (Appendix G.6), each condition is run at three seeds, and every accuracy is measured on speakers held out from training (Appendix G.2). Every number in the record and the paper comes from this study's runs, or from the previous study's record where a run is the previous study's; no pilot data is used.

### G.2 Task and data

The data are the previous study's ([Kryski 2026b](https://github.com/ekryski/oscillator-research/blob/main/papers/02-untrained-reservoirs/)): the 30,000 recordings of AudioMNIST ([Becker et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jfranklin.2023.11.038)), ten spoken digits by 60 speakers, resampled to 16 kHz, peak-normalized, trimmed of silence and capped at 1 s, in the same bank. Every result keeps speakers apart under its Protocol A: the arms are trained on speakers 1 to 48 and tested on speakers 49 to 60. The previous study's second protocol, on the published speaker folds, is not repeated here.

Recognition, the main task, names the digit of one recording. It is tested on all 6,000 recordings of the test speakers; a seed fixes a permutation of the training recordings, and the training set is its first 2,048. Chance is 10%.

The order task is the previous study's test of whether an arm carries the order of events. Two recordings of a digit pair (0 and 6, 1 and 8, 2 and 5, 3 and 7, 4 and 9), each drawn at random from the speakers' recordings of its digit, are joined into one clip behind a 272 ms lead-in with 100 ms between them, and the arm must say which came first. Each pair has 2,048 training clips from the training speakers, drawn afresh for each seed, and a fixed 2,048 test clips from the test speakers, balanced between the two orders. The clips are read over their whole span as one window (Appendix G.6), and the mean and spread of a signal do not depend on the order of its frames, so the input alone reads chance, 50%, and anything above it must come from the arm's own dynamics. The previous study's order clips and sets are reproduced bit for bit.

The digit-sequence task, new here, asks for more of that memory: the identity of every digit in a sequence, in its place. Two, three or four recordings of different digits, the digits drawn uniformly without repeats and each recording drawn from the speakers' recordings of its digit, are joined in the same way into clips of 2.37, 3.47 and 4.57 s (147, 216 and 284 frames), and the arm must name the digit at every position, each position by a readout of its own on the same features (Appendix G.6). Each length has 2,048 training clips per seed and a fixed 2,048 test clips, from the training and the test speakers respectively. Every position's digit is uniform over the ten, so chance at each position is exactly 10%, and chance of every position right is one in 90, 720 or 5,040 for two, three or four digits. The input read over the whole span can tell which digits a clip holds but hardly where: a reader that knows the digits but not their order reaches 1/L at each position of a sequence of L (50%, 33.3% and 25%) and gets every position right with probability 1/L! (50%, 16.7% and 4.2%), and these order-free levels, rather than chance, are what the input alone can be expected to reach. As in the previous study's order task, the trained baselines are not run on either memory task.

### G.3 Front end, band mapping and analysis window

Every arm hears the audio through the previous study's fixed front end: a log-mel spectrogram from a 512-sample (32 ms) Hann window every 256 samples (16 ms, 62.5 frames a second), rescaled by a fixed affine map, with nothing learned and no per-utterance normalization. Here its number of mel bands follows the lattice. A lattice of G rows is driven in one of two ways (Figure 1b): by G mel bands, one per row, or by the previous study's 16 bands mapped onto its rows, each band driving G/16 adjacent rows of a larger lattice and each row of the 8 × 8 lattice taking the mean of two adjacent bands. At 16 × 16 the two coincide. The first gives a larger lattice a finer input; the second holds the input fixed and varies only the network, so that between them the effect of the network's size can be told from the effect of the input's resolution. Nothing in either mapping is fitted.

At 8, 16 and 32 bands the front end is the previous study's exactly: a 512-point transform, 257 frequency bins. At 64 and 128 bands the narrowest mel filters fall between those bins, and at 128 bands one filter would be empty and 25 would touch a single bin. From 64 bands the transform is therefore zero-padded, to 1,024 points at 64 bands and 2,048 at 128, the lengths at which the narrowest filter spans three bins as the 32-band one does at 512. The window stays 512 samples and is aligned with the previous study's frames, so every band count has the previous study's frames, 61 for a 1 s clip, at the same times. Zero-padding samples the same windowed spectrum more finely and adds no frequency resolution, so neighbouring low bands at 64 and 128 carry strongly correlated energies. The analysis window is therefore a second front-end variable at those band counts: the lattices driven by their own 64 or 128 bands are also run, on recognition in the size, trained and quadrature experiments, under a real window as long as the transform, 1,024 samples (64 ms) and 2,048 (128 ms), which resolves the narrow bands at the cost of time resolution. Every frame stays centred where the previous study's is, so the frame count and the read are unchanged and only what each frame blends changes. The quadrature pathway takes its per-band phase from the same zero-padded transform, referenced to the start of the previous study's window, or from the longer window's own transform. Narrower bands hold less energy, so the drive's level falls slightly with band count: its mean at 0 dB is 1.38, 1.32, 1.24, 1.23 and 1.22 from 8 to 128 bands, on a range of 0 to about 1.6, and is not corrected.

### G.4 Network size: lattices, channels and the coupling ceiling

A network of C channels on a G × G lattice has C · G² oscillators and 2 · C · G² parameters: a coupling kernel of one weight per offset and a natural frequency per oscillator, per channel. With G from 8 to 128 and C from 1 to 16 it ranges from 64 oscillators and 128 parameters to 262,144 oscillators and 524,288 parameters.

Everything else about the network is the previous study's reference configuration: Kuramoto coupling on the torus, natural frequencies drawn per oscillator from N(1, 0.1²), restoring strength λ = 0.3, coupling ceiling 1, and one Euler step of 0.1 per frame, with every seed drawing the kernels and natural frequencies exactly as the previous study drew them. The natural frequencies' distribution does not depend on the lattice. The kernel's weights are drawn from N(0, 0.05²) at every size and each channel's kernel is then scaled, all its weights by one factor, so that the peak of its spectrum, the largest gain it applies to any spatial pattern of phases across the channel, equals the coupling ceiling. The previous study only lowered a kernel to the ceiling. At 16 × 16 that is the same rule, since a random kernel's peak there is always above it (1.42 to 2.54 over every geometry, seed and channel either study draws), and every 16 × 16 network, the coil and the cochleas included, is bit-identical under the two rules. At 8 × 8 it is not: the peak is usually below the ceiling (median 0.80), so lowering alone would have left the smallest networks with weaker coupling than the rest, for a reason that has nothing to do with size. Scaling to the ceiling exactly, up or down, holds the strongest coupling gain fixed at every size. What still changes with size is how that gain is spread: a random kernel's peak grows with the lattice (medians 0.80, 1.84, 4.0, 9.1 and 20.0 on the torus from 8 × 8 to 128 × 128), so a larger lattice's individual weights end up smaller, and the typical gain over all spatial patterns, the root mean square of the kernel's spectrum, drifts from 0.50 to 0.32. The per-weight initialization is not rescaled with G, since exact scaling already fixes the quantity the ceiling was meant to fix.

Every geometry is defined at every lattice size (Appendix F.2). The cube cuts each row of G oscillators into the most nearly square slab the row allows, and the coil and the cochleas are set by shares of the coil's length. The Stuart–Landau coupling functions run on the torus only, as in the previous study. The uncoupled network has no coupling term at all, so one per size and pathway serves every coupling function and geometry.

### G.5 Controls matched in size

Every network is compared with controls of its own size. The uncoupled oscillator network is the same network with its kernels set to zero; with no coupling term it does not depend on the coupling function or the geometry. The state-matched leaky-integrator bank is the previous study's bank at C channels of a G × G lattice: C · G² independent leaky integrators, each with a leak rate and an input weight (2 · C · G² parameters), band r driving every unit of row r, time constants spaced logarithmically from 16 ms to 1 s across the units of a row. The previous study's width-matched bank, with twice the units to match the network's two signals per oscillator, is the state-matched bank of twice the channels, which the size experiment runs up to 8 channels. The spectrogram-only baseline reads exactly the rows a lattice's networks are driven by.

The five trained baselines are the previous study's architectures and training recipe (AdamW, a learning rate of 3·10⁻³ annealed to 3·10⁻⁴, 30 epochs, batches of 64, a learned linear head on the shared statistics, standardized, and the ridge readout on the same statistics), each sized to the network it is compared with. Each has one width knob and the rest of its design fixed: the GRU's hidden size, the TCN's and the CNN's hidden channels, the transformer's model width with two heads, and the S4D's width, with as many states per channel as its width. The width is the widest whose parameter count does not exceed the network's; the CNN takes the narrowest that reaches it, as the previous study's CNN, one width over its budget, does. At 2,048 parameters on 16 rows this gives the previous study's baselines exactly (widths 18, 10, 13, 16 and 16; 1,840 to 2,109 parameters), and from 2,048 parameters up every baseline is within 15% of its network, with widths up to 358 at 524,288 parameters. Below 2,048 parameters the steps between widths are coarse, and in 10 of the 45 lattice, band-mapping and channel settings one or more baselines fall up to 52% under the network's count or 33% over it. Those runs are kept and flagged in the record, because the smallest sizes are where the study asks whether untrained oscillators are more useful than trained networks. A trained baseline is driven by exactly the rows its network is driven by, so it sees the same band mapping and window.

### G.6 Readout

Every arm is read the previous study's way. Each of its signals (a band energy, the sine or cosine of an oscillator's phase, a leaky integrator's state, or a trained baseline's hidden unit) is summarized by three statistics, its mean, its standard deviation and its mean absolute change from frame to frame, over each of four equal windows of frames 16 to 61, after a 16-frame warm-up, the same frames for every clip; on the memory tasks, over the whole span from frame 16 as one window. The spectrogram-only baseline is also read from frame 0, over the whole clip, its primary read. Every coupled and uncoupled network is also read with its rotation rates added, the previous study's secondary read: how far each oscillator's phase advances between frames, as the mean of cos Δθ and of sin Δθ over each window, two more numbers per oscillator and window. The arms' summaries range from 96 numbers (8 band energies) to 6,291,456 (a 128 × 128 network of 16 channels), so that readout capacity cannot pass for dynamics every arm is brought to the same width: its summaries are standardized with the training set's own statistics and multiplied by a random Gaussian matrix to 192 features, and to 1,024 and 4,096 as secondary widths, a projection that approximately preserves distances between feature vectors ([Johnson & Lindenstrauss 1984](https://doi.org/10.1090/conm/026/737400)). An arm is never read wider than its own number of features; at or above it, it is read unprojected.

A single linear layer then maps the 192 features to ten digit scores (two on the order task, and ten for each position, by a layer of its own, on the digit-sequence task), fitted by ridge regression, least squares with an L2 penalty solved in closed form, with the penalty chosen from four values on the last eighth of the training clips ([Jaeger 2001](https://www.ai.rug.nl/minds/uploads/EchoStatesTechRep.pdf); [Lukoševičius 2012](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-642-35289-8_36)). The readout's 1,930 weights are the same at every size: the network grows and its reader does not, so a gain with size belongs to the network. The trained baselines are trained end to end through a learned linear layer on the same statistics, standardized as the readout standardizes them, and then read by the same readout.

The projection matrix is drawn two ways, and every projected read is recorded under both. The fixed projection is the previous study's single draw, the same for every seed; the seeded projection is drawn afresh from each run's seed, so that the spread over seeds includes the projection's own variability, which a single draw leaves out. Both are reported.

A network of more than 4,096 states (from 32 × 32 at 8 channels) is too large to read at once: a 128 × 128 network of 16 channels has 6,291,456 features per clip, 202 GB for a run's 8,048 clips, and its projection matrix would be 103 GB. Because the channels do not act on each other and the standardization is per feature, the read splits exactly by channel: each channel is simulated alone, its training features are standardized with their own statistics and projected, and the projections are added, the test clips a batch at a time and one read at a time. Given the same matrix this reproduces the in-memory read's accuracies exactly in every tested case, the projected features agreeing to floating-point rounding. The matrix rows each channel meets are drawn from seeds of their own, with the same distribution and scale, since reaching a channel's rows of a single draw would mean drawing all of it. Networks of 4,096 states or fewer, including every run the previous study made, are read in memory with its code.

### G.7 Synchronization and locking

Every coupled and uncoupled network run also records how synchronized its oscillators are and how closely they follow the input, over the test clips and the frames it is read over. The order parameter R is the magnitude of the mean of e^{iθ} over a channel's oscillators, averaged over channels and frames: 1 when every oscillator of a channel shares one phase, near 0 when their phases are spread ([Kuramoto 1975](https://doi.org/10.1007/BFb0013365); [Strogatz 2000](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0167-2789%2800%2900094-4)). Each oscillator's locking to its input is the phase-locking value between its phase and the phase φ of its own row's drive, the magnitude of the mean over frames of e^{i(θ − φ)}, averaged over oscillators ([Pikovsky et al. 2001](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511755743)); φ is the band's own phase on the quadrature pathway and the analytic phase of the band's energy on the spectrogram pathway. An oscillator counts as entrained when its locking value exceeds 0.5, and the share entrained is recorded with the mean amplitude, which is 1 except under the Stuart–Landau function with free amplitude. These are the previous study's instruments. Nothing reads them, no accuracy depends on them, and a network read channel by channel records the values it records when read at once. They are reported beside each network's accuracy at every size, and in the design and cochlea experiments for every coupling function and geometry.

### G.8 Input pathways

The audio drives a reservoir in the previous study's two ways ([Kryski 2026b](https://github.com/ekryski/oscillator-research/blob/main/papers/02-untrained-reservoirs/)). **Spectrogram**: each band energy adds to the rotation rate of the oscillators in its row, θ̇ᵢ += g·Aᵣ(t); every experiment but the quadrature ones uses it. **Quadrature**: each band's energy together with the phase of its signal relative to the band's centre frequency, entering as a phase-referenced push g·Aᵣ sin(φᵣ − θᵢ) ([Adler 1946](https://doi.org/10.1109/JRPROC.1946.229930)); at 64 and 128 bands the phase is taken from the zero-padded transform, referenced to the start of the same window, or from the longer window's own transform. The leaky-integrator bank has no phase for the quadrature pathway to act on and is not run on it. Each pathway's spectrogram-only baseline reads that pathway's own front end.

### G.9 Noise protocol

White noise is added to every clip, in training and in test alike, at a signal-to-noise ratio of 0 dB: as loud as the clip's own speech. Each clip's noise is drawn from a generator seeded by the clip's identity, so every arm hears exactly the same noisy clip. The previous study also ran clean audio, on which the task nearly saturates, and noise 5 dB louder than the speech (−5 dB); the size study runs 0 dB alone, the lower of the previous study's two noise levels, at which the task does not saturate, so that the design stays affordable across its lattice sizes.

### G.10 Input gain and the integrator-validity bound

Input gain scales the input before it drives a reservoir, and applies only to the reservoirs, as in the previous study. Every reservoir here runs at gain 1, where the band energies push with about the magnitude of the natural frequencies. The spectrogram-only baseline has no dynamics for a gain to act on, and a trained baseline learns its own input scale.

The network advances in steps of 0.1, and the phase an oscillator gains in one step must stay below π for the step to be valid ([Hairer et al. 1993](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-540-78862-1)). At gain 1 the input adds at most about 0.15 radian a step. The coupling adds at most 0.1 radian a step in root mean square over a channel at every lattice size, because exact scaling holds the kernel's largest gain at the ceiling of 1; without a ceiling, a random kernel's gain would grow with the lattice, as its spectral peak does (Appendix G.4). The forward pass stays finite on every geometry and coupling function at 8 × 8 and 64 × 64, and on the coil and the cochleas at every lattice size, as tested.

### G.11 Runs taken from the previous study, and the checks

The previous study ran the 16 × 16 lattice, one band per row and its 512-sample window, at 4 channels. Wherever a run here is a run it recorded (the same task and set, arm, pathway, noise level, input gain and seed), its cells are taken from its record rather than run again, under the same run identity and label: the spectrogram-only baselines, the reference network, its uncoupled copy and both banks of its controls experiment, on recognition and the order task; its trained baselines at 2,048 parameters; the configurations of its design experiment; the diagonal of its quadrature experiment; and its coil and cochleas. That is 234 runs. The previous study recorded most of them under its fixed projection only, and its projection experiment reread its controls experiment's reservoirs under both projections but without their rotation rates. A run is taken from its record only if every projected cell reported here for it has its seeded counterpart there: 66 are (the spectrogram-only baselines, the banks, and the trained baselines read unprojected). The other 168 are run again here, on the CPU, and that run is the one reported; its fixed cells are the previous study's.

That reuse rests on three things. Exact kernel scaling leaves every 16 × 16 network bit-identical to the previous study's (Appendix G.4). The arms involved have at most 4,096 states and are read by the previous study's own code, on the order task from its own clips. And the reuse check reruns a sample of the previous study's runs with this study's code, on the CPU: one run of each arm kind from each of its record files taken from (its controls, design, cochlea and quadrature experiments), and the GRU, TCN, CNN and S4D, whose recipe the previous study fixed and whose runs it made again. Every cell the two records share is compared, accuracy and per-clip correctness; the summary reports how many are identical and lists any that are not. The reuse check's runs are recorded apart, and nothing reported comes from them.

The leak check reads the coupled and uncoupled networks and the state-matched bank, at 1 and 16 channels of every lattice, with no input: at input gain 0 every clip's trajectory is the same, so every cell should read exactly chance, 10%, both in memory and channel by channel. The summary reports every cell that does not. Neither check scores a result; both check the pipeline, and a result a check concerns is not reported until its cause is found and fixed.

### G.12 Reporting and uncertainty

No threshold decides a result. Every accuracy is reported as its mean over three seeds, with the sample standard deviation and each seed's value, under the fixed and under the seeded projection. A seed sets an arm's random parameters, which training clips it draws and, under the seeded projection, the projection. Every comparison between two arms is paired: both are scored on the same test clips at the same task, lattice, band mapping, analysis window, channel count, seed and projection, and it is reported as the mean difference in accuracy, the standard deviation of that difference over seeds, and a 95% interval from resampling the test clips 2,000 times. The standard deviation over seeds is how much a result moves when the random draws change; the interval is how precisely the fixed test set measures a difference. The primary cell for every comparison is width 192, 2,048 training clips and the task's primary read, the four-window read for recognition and the whole-span read for the memory tasks. Every accuracy is reported beside its task's chance and, on the digit-sequence task, its order-free levels; the order task is also reported with its five pairs pooled, and the digit-sequence task per position, as the mean over positions and as every position right at once. The coil and the cochleas are compared with the torus, the helix and each other over the four phase coupling functions, matched on coupling function at each size, as the previous study pairs them. A network's rotation rates are reported as its accuracy with them against without them, and its synchronization and locking as their mean and standard deviation over seeds. Accuracy is reported against the number of oscillators and against parameters, with the layouts of equal size marked. The runs taken from the previous study are marked wherever they appear, and every run's device is in the record.
