\begin{abstract}
Coupled-oscillator networks are gaining interest as a machine-learning
substrate, on the premise that oscillator physics turns signals into
useful structure, yet the ablations that would isolate what the dynamics
contribute are rarely run. We run them for untrained coupled oscillator
networks on spoken-digit recognition, on AudioMNIST with held-out
speakers and white noise as loud as the speech and 5 dB louder. A
network of 1,024 oscillators is read by a closed- form linear readout at
a common width of 192 features and compared with the same readout on its
input alone, the same network uncoupled, a leaky-integrator bank of the
same size, an echo state network and a random oscillators network
selected on validation speakers, and five trained networks of the same
parameter count, across six coupling functions, eight lattice
geometries, three kinds of natural frequencies, two input pathways and
sweeps of gain, restoring strength, coupling ceiling, readout width and
training size. Three findings. Most of the accuracy is the front end and
the readout: in noise the untrained Kuramoto network reads within a
point of its own spectrogram (77.8\% against 78.0\% at 0 dB) and below
it on clean audio. What the dynamics add is memory: the network recovers
most of what the input loses without its onset and carries the order of
two digits that an order-free read of the input cannot, but a bank of
leaky integrators does both, so the memory is integration rather than
oscillation. The standard reservoirs read within 1.7 points of the input
and a memoryless random expansion of it read 19 points below the input.
A free amplitude, expressed in a Stuart--Landau oscillator network, is
the one design factor that lifts the network above its input in noise,
by 1.1 to 2.3 points, and holds its accuracy to an input gain of 8 where
phase oscillators lose 15 to 19 points, a robustness that survives an
exact-rotation drive step. Coupling function, lattice geometry and
natural frequencies each move accuracy by less than a point. Among the
untrained arms the echo state network reads highest on clean audio and
matches the leaky-integrator bank on order, though it reads just below
the input in noise. These results are the untrained baselines a trained
oscillator network of this size should beat.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec-1}

The oscillation and synchronization recorded in neural populations as
they track speech
\citep{assaneo2020, doelling2023, pittman-polletta2021, dogonasheva2026}
are consistent with neurons acting, in part, as coupled oscillators. We
do not test that conjecture here. It motivates a narrower question:
where are the useful boundaries of oscillatory neural networks, and what
do their dynamics contribute?

A recent survey of oscillator networks in machine learning
\citep{kryski2026} found the ablations that would answer that question
largely missing: the coupling term had been removed as a single variable
only three times, coupling functions had been compared only within the
Kuramoto family \citep{nunley2026}. One graph network has since set
Stuart--Landau against Kuramoto, without fixing the amplitude
\citep{zhang2026}. No study varied the lattice geometry or its
boundaries as a single variable at a matched budget, and none contrasted
different audio input structures under controls. We address those gaps
on one task, spoken-digit recognition, where automatic speech
recognition began \citep{davis1952} and a standard benchmark for
oscillator reservoirs built in hardware
\citep{torrejon2017, shougat2023}. An untrained coupled oscillator
network, read by a linear readout, is tested under six coupling
functions and eight lattice geometries, among them a coil and a cochlea,
and compared with the readout applied to its input alone, the same
network uncoupled, leaky-integrator banks of the same size and five
trained networks of the same parameter count, on AudioMNIST
\citep{becker2024} with held-out speakers and added noise. Untrained
coupled oscillators have recognized spoken digits before, in simulation
and on TI-46 with the same speakers in training and test
\citep{opala2019, coulombe2017}, and the two noisy AudioMNIST studies we
found also share speakers \citep{beoletto2026, young2026}. To our
knowledge none has been tested on held-out speakers with added noise
against its input alone and its own uncoupled form.

The paper makes four contributions. First, a controlled ablation set for
an untrained oscillator network, 6,353 runs over six coupling functions,
eight lattice geometries, three kinds of natural frequencies, two input
pathways and sweeps of gain, restoring strength, coupling ceiling,
readout width and training size, each compared with controls that
isolate the input, the readout's width, the memory and the coupling.
Second, a speaker-disjoint, noisy AudioMNIST protocol with an order task
that order-free readouts cannot solve. Third, the isolation of the
oscillator's amplitude as the one design factor that lifts the network
above its input in noise, by holding every other draw fixed and removing
only that degree of freedom. Fourth, the standard reservoir baselines
(an echo state network and a random oscillators network) with
hyperparameters selected on validation speakers, a validation-selected
configuration of the network itself, and two alternatives to the random
projection, so that the comparison does not rest on one reader.
\hyperref[sec-4]{Section 4} opens with the findings and presents each
comparison as evidence for them.

\section{Background}\label{sec-2}

Coupled oscillators synchronize, form clusters, lock to a drive or split
into coherent and incoherent domains, depending on how they are coupled
and arranged
\citep{pikovsky2001, strogatz2000, kuramoto2002, abrams2004}. Machine
learning has used them two ways. Untrained, as reservoirs read by a
trained linear readout \citep{jaeger2001, maass2002, tanaka2019}: a
spintronic oscillator recognized spoken digits in hardware
\citep{torrejon2017}, though much of that accuracy came from its
cochlear front end \citep{abreu-araujo2020}, whose authors therefore
report a reservoir's gain over its front end alone and test it on
held-out speakers in noise on another corpus. On AudioMNIST, a simulated
network of 128 coupled protonic nickelate devices, a neuromorphic
element that does not oscillate, read above the same network uncoupled
and above a readout of the unprocessed input \citep{zhou2026}, which
inspired one of the controls used in our study. Trained oscillatory
neural networks (ONNs), structured as layers whose coupling or
frequencies are learned, have been applied to various tasks: Neural Wave
Machines, an oscillator lattice, on sequence classification and
forecasting simple physical systems \citep{keller2023}, AKOrN on
unsupervised object discovery, adversarial robustness, uncertainty
calibration and Sudoku \citep{miyato2025}, WONN on image recognition,
Sudoku and maze solving \citep{dai2026}, FSN on character-level language
modelling \citep{nunley2026}, Un-0 on image generation
\citep{unconv-ai2026} and coupled oscillatory recurrent networks, coRNN
\citep{rusch2021}, one of which reached 89\% on AudioMNIST from the raw
waveform with a random, not speaker-disjoint, split
\citep{chandravadia2026}. Most adopt one coupling function and one
arrangement, and the survey found that few share a benchmark, AKOrN and
WONN on Sudoku among them, and fewer share a protocol
\citep{kryski2026}. Here one task is held fixed and the coupling
function, the geometry and the input are varied across it, on untrained
networks, so that each can be compared with controls in future training
experimentation.

\section{Methods}\label{sec-3}

\subsection{Design}\label{sec-3-1}

This study asks whether an untrained coupled oscillator network carries
information for recognizing spoken digits beyond its input alone, beyond
a non-oscillating memory of the same size and beyond the same
oscillators uncoupled; how trained networks of the same parameter count
compare; whether the coupling function, lattice geometry, natural
frequencies, restoring strength, coupling ceiling, input pathway and
input gain change the answer; how the answer changes with the readout's
width and the training data; and whether an untrained network can
\emph{remember} the order of events. It ran as eight experiments, 6,353
runs, each one arm at one noise level, input gain and seed, every
condition at three seeds. The code and every run's record are in the
supplementary material, and \hyperref[sec-A]{Appendix A} defines every
term.

Every oscillator network is 4 channels of a 16 \ensuremath{\times} 16
lattice, 1,024 oscillators, and each oscillator's phase evolves as
\ensuremath{\dot{\theta}_{i}} = \ensuremath{\omega_{i}} +
coupling\ensuremath{_{i}} + g\ensuremath{\cdot}u\ensuremath{_{i}}
\ensuremath{-} \ensuremath{\lambda} sin \ensuremath{\theta_{i}}: its
natural frequency, the coupling from the other oscillators of its
channel through a random kernel whose spectrum is capped at the
\emph{coupling ceiling}, the input at gain g, and a restoring pull of
strength \ensuremath{\lambda}. Row r of every channel is driven by mel
band r. The six coupling functions (Kuramoto, Kuramoto--Sakaguchi,
second harmonic, Winfree, and Stuart--Landau with and without its
amplitude) differ only in the coupling term, and a lattice geometry
changes only which oscillators are neighbours (see
\hyperref[sec-B]{Appendices B} and \hyperref[sec-C]{C}). The reference
configuration is Kuramoto coupling on the torus with random natural
frequencies, restoring strength 0.3 and coupling ceiling 1. Kuramoto is
the reference coupling because it is the most widely used in the prior
art, and the torus the reference geometry because it wraps in both
directions, so no oscillator sits at an edge. The configuration was
fixed in advance and then checked by a calibration on validation
speakers (noise levels, gain, restoring strength and coupling ceiling),
with which it largely agrees (\hyperref[sec-F]{Appendix F}).
\hyperref[sec-4-8]{Section 4.8} reports the configuration that
calibration selects beside it. \hyperref[sec-E]{Appendix E} gives every
reservoir's discrete update, solver, time step and initial state.

The arms compared with it are the \textbf{spectrogram-only baseline},
the readout applied directly to the input; the \textbf{uncoupled
oscillator network}, the same network with its coupling kernels set to
zero; two untrained \textbf{leaky-integrator banks}, one matched to the
network's 1,024 states and 2,048 parameters and one to its 2,048 exposed
signals; and five \textbf{trained baselines} (GRU, a dilated residual
TCN, CNN, transformer and S4D) of 1,840 to 2,109 parameters, trained end
to end (AdamW, 30 epochs) through a learned linear layer on the same
statistics the readout reads, standardized. The four untrained dynamical
arms are the reservoirs. Three \textbf{reservoir baselines} of the same
state count complete the comparison (\hyperref[sec-G]{Appendix G}): a
leaky echo state network, the random oscillators network of
\citet{ceni2024}, each with hyperparameters selected on validation
speakers, and a memoryless random nonlinear expansion of the input.

\subsection{Task, data and noise}\label{sec-3-2}

We perform single-digit audio classification over AudioMNIST
\citep{becker2024}, which holds 30,000 recordings of digits 0 through 9
by 60 speakers. Every result keeps speakers apart to avoid a model
overfitting to a particular speaker's vocal spectrogram. Under Protocol
A the arms train on speakers 1 to 48 and test on all 6,000 recordings of
speakers 49 to 60, an 80/20 split by speaker, with training sets of
2,048 clips (8,192 and 24,000 in the training-size comparison). Wherever
a configuration or a hyperparameter is selected (the calibration of the
reference, the selected network and the reservoir baselines), speakers
41 to 48 are held out of training as validation speakers, so nothing is
selected on the test speakers (\hyperref[sec-F]{Appendix F}). Under
Protocol B the arms follow Becker et al.'s five speaker folds
(60/20/20). Because the clean task nearly saturates, white noise is
added to every clip at signal-to-noise ratios of 0 dB (as loud as the
speech) and \ensuremath{-}5 dB (5 dB louder), the levels at which
\citet{young2026} trained hybrid real and complex-valued convolutional
networks on AudioMNIST, though they did so on a random audio sample
split so were not speaker disjoint. We also report clean audio for
reference. Additionally, we performed an order task on five different
digit pairs where two digits of a pair are spoken one after the other
and the arm must say which came first. Statistics of an input read over
the whole span cannot tell the orders apart, so the spectrogram-only
baseline reads chance, 50\%. Every arm hears the same fixed front end, a
16-band log-mel spectrogram at 62.5 frames per second, without training
on this task besides the single-digit training methodology.

\subsection{Readout}\label{sec-3-3}

Every arm is read the same way (\hyperref[sec-D-1]{Appendix D.1} draws
the readout for each kind of arm). Each of its signals (a band energy,
the sine or cosine of an oscillator's phase, a leaky integrator's state
or a trained baseline's hidden unit) is summarized by its mean, standard
deviation and mean absolute change over each of four equal windows of
frames 16 to 61, after a 16-frame warm-up (frames 16 to 147 as one
window for the order task). So that readout capacity cannot obscure
dynamics, every arm is read at the same width, 192 standardized
features. That is exactly the spectrogram-only baseline's own number of
features (16 mel bands by 3 statistics by 4 windows), so the input is
read without compression, and it is the native width of four of the five
trained baselines. Arms with more features, the reservoirs (12,288 to
24,576) and the GRU (216), are projected down to 192 by a random
Gaussian matrix \citep{johnson1984}. Arms with fewer features, the
baseline and the trained baselines on the order task (48 to 54), are
read as they are. The features are classified by one linear layer fitted
by ridge regression in closed form, the reservoir-computing standard
\citep{jaeger2001, lukosevicius2012} and the weakest reader that can be
fitted identically for every arm. The projection matrix is drawn once
from a fixed seed and serves every run of the same native width, so the
spread over seeds leaves out the projection's own randomness. The
controls experiment's reservoirs are also read through a matrix drawn
from each run's seed. The spectrogram-only baseline is read over the
whole clip, its primary form, and from frame 16, the frames every other
arm is read over. \hyperref[sec-4-8]{Section 4.8} reads the reservoirs
two more ways at width 192, through a PCA fitted on the training clips
and through a structured pooling within each lattice row, so that the
conclusions do not rest on one random matrix.

\subsection{Input pathways and gain}\label{sec-3-4}

The audio drives a reservoir in one of two ways: the
\textbf{spectrogram} pathway adds each band's energy to the rotation
rate of its row's oscillators, and the \textbf{quadrature} pathway adds
a phase-referenced push g\ensuremath{\cdot}A\ensuremath{_{r}}
sin(\ensuremath{\varphi_{r}} \ensuremath{-} \ensuremath{\theta_{i}}),
the injection form of \citet{adler1946}, so an oscillator can lock to
its band's own cycle. Only the coupled networks run on the quadrature
pathway, beside its own spectrogram-only baseline, which reads the
quadrature front end directly: a leaky integrator has no phase for the
push to act on, the trained baselines take the input as features rather
than as a drive, and the uncoupled network was left out because the
question is whether coupled oscillators can use a phase-referenced drive
at all. Input gain scales the input into a reservoir. It is run at gains
of 1 and 2 throughout and swept from 0.25 to 12 for the reference
configuration of each coupling function, below the integrator's bound
\citep{hairer1993}. The spectrogram-only baseline and the trained
baselines do not have gain applied because the readout's standardization
removes any fixed scale from the directly read spectrogram input, and
the trained models learn their own.

\subsection{Reporting}\label{sec-3-5}

Every task accuracy result reported is the mean over replicates (three
seeds; five folds under Protocol B) \ensuremath{\pm} the sample standard
deviation, and every comparison between two arms is paired on the same
test clips, condition and seed, reported as the mean difference
\ensuremath{\pm} its standard deviation over seeds and a 95\% interval
from 2,000 paired bootstrap resamples of the test clips. Every
comparison is reported at the primary cell, the four-window read at
2,048 training clips and readout width 192, unless it says otherwise.

\section{Results}\label{sec-4}

\textbf{Findings.} Three findings organize the comparisons below. (1)
Most of every arm's accuracy comes from the front end and the readout:
at the common width the untrained Kuramoto network reads within a point
of its own spectrogram in noise and 2.5 points below it on clean audio
(\hyperref[sec-4-1]{Section 4.1}), and the input's format matters more
than the network's design, since the same networks read near chance on
the quadrature pathway (\hyperref[sec-4-6]{Section 4.6}). (2) What the
dynamics add is memory. Read only after its warm-up, the network
recovers most of what the input loses without its onset, and it carries
the order of two digits that an order-free read of the input cannot see
(\hyperref[sec-4-2]{Section 4.2}). A leaky-integrator bank of the same
size does both, so the memory is integration, which oscillation also
performs, rather than oscillation itself. Coupling adds about a point on
noisy recognition and 2 to 19 points on order. (3) A free amplitude is
the one design factor that lifts the network above its input in noise,
by 1.1 to 2.3 points, and the one that keeps its accuracy as the input
gain rises (\hyperref[sec-4-3]{Sections 4.3} and
\hyperref[sec-4-5]{4.5}). The coupling function, the lattice geometry
and the natural frequencies each move accuracy by less than a point
(\hyperref[sec-4-3]{Sections 4.3} and \hyperref[sec-4-4]{4.4}).
\hyperref[sec-4-7]{Section 4.7} shows what the common width costs, and
\hyperref[sec-4-8]{Section 4.8} sets the network beside the standard
reservoir baselines, a configuration selected on validation speakers,
and two other readers.

\subsection{The network against its controls and the trained
baselines}\label{sec-4-1}

\hyperref[fig-fig01-sec4-1-recognition-arms]{Figure 1} shows every arm
at the primary cell, and the supplementary material gives the tables.
The spectrogram-only baseline read 93.6\%, 78.0\% and 71.6\% on clean
audio, at 0 dB and at \ensuremath{-}5 dB, and the Kuramoto network at
gain 1 read 91.1\%, 77.8\% and 70.7\%. That is within a point of its
input with noise (\ensuremath{-}0.23 {[}\ensuremath{-}0.99, +0.52{]} at
0 dB, \ensuremath{-}0.88 {[}\ensuremath{-}1.66, \ensuremath{-}0.09{]} at
\ensuremath{-}5 dB) and 2.5 points below it on clean audio, but 7.5 to
15.9 points above the input read over the same frames, from frame 16.
Coupling added 0.4 to 1.1 points over the uncoupled network. The
state-matched leaky-integrator bank read about 4 points below the
network with noise and 2.4 above it on clean audio. The Stuart--Landau
network read level with its input on clean audio and above it in every
noisy condition (80.3\% and 73.0\% at gain 1; \hyperref[sec-4-3]{Section
4.3}). With noise every trained baseline read above the Kuramoto
network, the GRU highest at 82.8\% and 76.9\%, with the Stuart--Landau
network in their range. On the speaker folds of \citet{becker2024}
(clean, 18,000 training clips), the baseline read 96.0 \ensuremath{\pm}
1.7\%, the network 91.9 \ensuremath{\pm} 3.1\% (96.9 \ensuremath{\pm}
1.3\% at width 4,096) and the trained baselines 97.0\% to 98.4\%,
against 95.8\% reported for AlexNet on spectrograms.

\subsection{Coupling function}\label{sec-4-3}

The design experiment crossed six coupling functions, six lattice
geometries, three kinds of natural frequencies, two restoring strengths
and two coupling ceilings (the Stuart--Landau functions on the torus
only), 312 configurations at 0 and \ensuremath{-}5 dB, gains 1 and 2 and
three seeds, each level paired with its reference over every
configuration matching it in the other factors
(\hyperref[fig-fig03-sec4-3-coupling-functions]{Figure 3}). The
\textbf{Stuart--Landau} network, whose oscillators have a free
amplitude, read 2.1 to 3.8 points above its Kuramoto match, the largest
difference in the experiment, and at the reference configuration it read
above the whole-clip baseline in all four noisy conditions, by 1.14 to
2.31 points with every interval above zero. With its amplitude fixed it
read within 0.3 points of Kuramoto. The other phase coupling functions
differed from Kuramoto by at most 0.46 points. On clean audio it read
2.8 points above Kuramoto at gain 1 and 6.1 at gain 2 and level with the
input, and with its amplitude fixed within 0.1 points of Kuramoto, so

\subsection{Lattice geometry and natural frequencies}\label{sec-4-4}

Every \textbf{lattice geometry} in the design experiment read within
0.65 points of the torus, the sphere and sheet lowest
(\hyperref[fig-fig04-sec4-4-lattice-geometries]{Figure 7},
\hyperref[sec-C]{Appendix C}). A \textbf{coil}, one open line from base
to apex with one octave per turn, read within 0.22 points of the torus.
A \textbf{cochlea}, the coil with coupling three times as strong from
base to apex as back and growing with the coil's curvature toward the
apex, read 0.41 to 0.97 points below the torus. Part of that gap is the
cochlea's weaker coupling: capping its curvature weights at the apex
halves its average coupling, and a control at the coil's average
coupling read 0.28 to 0.71 points above the cochlea, yet 0.40 to 0.47
points below the coil at gain 1 and within 0.12 points of it at gain 2.

Each oscillator's \textbf{natural frequency} is its rate of rotation
when nothing acts on it. The reference draws each at random with mean 1
and standard deviation 0.1, unrelated to the band that drives it.
\textbf{Tonotopic} frequencies order the rates by band, as the cochlea
orders pitch by place: the rate rises a quarter octave per row, from
0.41 in the row of the lowest mel band to 5.5 in the highest, jittered
by 5\%. \textbf{Identical} frequencies set every rate to 1. Tonotopic
frequencies read 0.38 to 1.16 percentage points below random ones, and
identical ones within 0.1
(\hyperref[fig-fig05-sec4-4-natural-frequencies]{Figure 8},
\hyperref[sec-C]{Appendix C}).

\subsection{Restoring strength, coupling ceiling and
gain}\label{sec-4-5}

A restoring strength (the pull toward phase 0) of 0.1 read 0.13 to 0.23
points below 0.3, and a coupling ceiling of 0.5 read 0.39 to 0.68 points
below 1. A sweep beyond these levels, for every coupling function at the
reference configuration, moved accuracy by at most 2.8 points
(\hyperref[fig-fig06-sec4-5-restoring-ceiling-gain]{Figure 4}). Gain
separates the Stuart--Landau network from the rest. From gain 1 to gain
8 it lost 4.3 points at 0 dB and 2.8 at \ensuremath{-}5 dB, while every
phase-oscillator network lost 15 to 19 points and the Stuart--Landau
network with its amplitude fixed 25 to 28. Beyond gain 8 it also gave
way, losing 20.8 points by gain 12 at 0 dB. Below gain 1 the order
reverses: at gains 0.25 and 0.5 the phase-oscillator networks read 0.3
to 2.5 points above gain 1 and the Stuart--Landau network 0.7 to 1.7
points below it, which brings the best phase network within 0.8 points
of the Stuart--Landau network at gain 1 at 0 dB and level with it at
\ensuremath{-}5 dB.

The fixed-amplitude network's fall below Kuramoto at high gain has a
numerical cause. The Stuart--Landau update applies the input inside a
semi-implicit step that rotates the state by
2\ensuremath{\cdot}arcsin(h/2) per frame rather than h, with h = dt
\ensuremath{\times} gain \ensuremath{\times} input
(\hyperref[sec-E]{Appendix E}). The error is within 0.4\% at gains 1 and
2, where the two agree, but 7\% by gain 8 and 24\% by gain 12. The
exact-drive experiment reruns both Stuart--Landau networks with the
input applied as an exact rotation at every gain (dashed in
\hyperref[fig-fig06-sec4-5-restoring-ceiling-gain]{Figure 4}). With the
exact drive the fixed-amplitude network reads within 0.8 points of
Kuramoto at every gain, and the free-amplitude network loses only 2.5
points from gain 1 to gain 8 at 0 dB and 1.1 at \ensuremath{-}5 dB,
where the fixed-amplitude network loses 17.6 and 15.2. The free
amplitude's robustness to gain is the oscillator's and not the
integrator's.

\subsection{Quadrature pathway}\label{sec-4-6}

The quadrature experiment ran ten networks on the quadrature pathway
(the four phase coupling functions on the torus, and Kuramoto on the
helix, each with random and with tonotopic natural frequencies) at 0 and
\ensuremath{-}5 dB, gains 1 and 2 and three seeds. All ten read 11.7\%
to 15.1\%, near the chance level of 10\% and 55.7 to 66.0 points below
the same networks on the spectrogram pathway, while the spectrogram-only
baseline read on the quadrature front end reached 36.1\% at 0 dB. In no
run did more than 0.3\% of the oscillators lock to their band.

\subsection{Readout width and training data}\label{sec-4-7}

Widening the readout to 1,024 and 4,096 features raised nearly every arm
with more than 192 features to read, the Kuramoto network by up to 3.8
points at gain 1 (6.3 at gain 2) and the leaky-integrator banks by up to
6.2, and left the 192-wide arms unchanged
(\hyperref[fig-fig07-sec4-7-readout-width]{Figure 10},
\hyperref[sec-D-1]{Appendix D.1}). From 2,048 to 24,000 training clips,
at width 192 and with noise, the spectrogram-only baseline gained 3.5 to
4.1 points, the Kuramoto network 2.0 and the trained baselines 7.1 to
12.4, since they learn through their networks as well as their readout.
At width 4,096 the Kuramoto network read above the whole-clip
spectrogram-only baseline at every training size, and with 2,048 clips
at 0 dB above the S4D, CNN and transformer (81.4\% against 79.2\% to
80.8\%). With 8,192 or 24,000 clips every trained baseline read above
every reservoir at every width with noise. Read through a projection
drawn from each run's seed instead of the fixed one, the reservoirs
moved by \ensuremath{-}1.3 to +1.2 points and every paired comparison
kept its sign.

\subsection{Reservoir baselines, model selection and the
projection}\label{sec-4-8}

The echo state network and the random oscillators network, at the
hyperparameters the validation speakers selected
(\hyperref[sec-G]{Appendix G}), read like arms already in the study
(\hyperref[fig-fig13-sec4-8-reservoir-baselines]{Figure 16} and the
table in \hyperref[sec-G]{Appendix G}). The echo state network is the
best untrained arm on clean audio, 95.3\% against the input's 93.6\%,
and matches the leaky-integrator bank on order (98.9\% to 99.9\%). In
noise it reads 77.2\% at 0 dB and 70.9\% at \ensuremath{-}5 dB, just
below the input, as the Kuramoto network does. The random oscillators
network reads 79.3\% and 73.3\% in noise, above the input and in the
Stuart--Landau network's range, but 87.9\% on clean audio. The
memoryless random nonlinear expansion of the input reads 18 to 20 points
below the input in noise and chance on order. So what the reservoirs add
to the input is memory, not more nonlinear features of the same frame,
and the standard reservoirs add no more of it than the lattice does.

Selected on the validation speakers (\hyperref[sec-F]{Appendix F}), the
Kuramoto network's configuration reads 78.8\% and 72.5\% on the test
speakers, 1.3 and 1.5 points above the reference trained on the same
speakers. The Stuart--Landau network's selection reads half a point
below its reference at 0 dB and is the reference itself at
\ensuremath{-}5 dB. The selected Kuramoto network still reads below the
Stuart--Landau network at its reference.

Read through a PCA fitted on the training clips instead of the random
matrix, the coupled networks and the banks gain 1 to 3.6 points in
noise, and the comparisons that decide the paper read as they do through
the random matrix: at gain 1 the coupled network reads 2.5 and 2.4
points above the uncoupled network (0 and \ensuremath{-}5 dB) and the
Stuart--Landau network 2.0 and 2.2 above Kuramoto. Through the
within-row pooling, which averages the 128 signals of a row into one
number per statistic and window, the differences shrink, and the
Stuart--Landau network's lead remains only at gain 2 (2.6 and 3.4
points). So what coupling and the amplitude add is carried partly by
structure within a row, between channels, columns or sine and cosine,
that a random matrix and a PCA both keep and an average over the row
removes (\hyperref[sec-D]{Appendix D}).

\section{Discussion and conclusion}\label{sec-5}

\textbf{What the dynamics add.} Finding 2 rests on three observations. A
Kuramoto oscillator's phase advances at its natural frequency plus its
drive, so, apart from the restoring pull and the coupling, its phase is
the running sum of its band's energy wrapped around the circle. A leaky
integrator keeps a running average of the same energy. Because an
integrator's state at each moment depends on what came before,
statistics of that state over the audio clip differ with the order of
its inputs, where the same statistics of the input do not. That is why
both remember order and the input alone cannot. Coupling also moves
information across the lattice: read through only its upper eight rows,
the coupled network reads 28 to 31 points above the uncoupled one, whose
upper rows carry no more than the input's upper bands
(\hyperref[sec-D-2]{Appendix D.2}). The analysis of variance per band
and time window (\hyperref[sec-D-2]{Appendix D.2},
\hyperref[fig-fig11-appD2-class-information]{Figures 11} and
\hyperref[fig-fig12-appD2-class-information-seeds]{12}) shows the
input's class information peaking at the onset and every reservoir
carrying it into later windows. The results align with the reports that
much of a reservoir's accuracy is its front end, for a spintronic
oscillator on TI-46 \citep{abreu-araujo2020} and for a piezoelectric
reservoir on AudioMNIST, which matched logistic regression on its input
\citep{buckley2026}.

\textbf{Network design.} A free amplitude is where the design effect
sits, not the Stuart--Landau coupling form: with its amplitude fixed,
the network reads like Kuramoto. On graphs, a Stuart--Landau network
also reads above its Kuramoto counterpart \citep{zhang2026}, but there
the two are separately built and trained networks whose coupling,
parameters and training all differ. Here the fixed-amplitude variant is
the free one with the same random kernel, natural frequencies, initial
state, input, solver and readout and one degree of freedom removed, so
the contrast isolates the amplitude. Four experiments in
\hyperref[sec-D-2]{Appendix D.2} say in part why the amplitude helps.
Read through its amplitude alone (\textbar z\textbar{} per oscillator,
half the features), the Stuart--Landau network keeps its whole lead over
Kuramoto, and read through its phase alone it loses most of that lead,
so what the readout gains is the amplitude itself as a signal. The gain
sits in the upper rows, which receive only a noise-dominated drive and
are driven mainly by their neighbours: read one band at a time, they are
the best single-band readouts of any arm in noise (51\% at 0 dB against
44\% for Kuramoto's rows and 25\% for the input's own upper bands). The
amplitude need not be slow, since a network whose amplitude relaxes
sixteen times faster reads the same at gain 1. Our reading is that the
radial equation makes each oscillator's radius a record of the radial
force on it: the coupling's pull toward its neighbours' mean, large and
inward when the oscillator is out of step with them, and the restoring
pull toward phase 0. The readout then receives each oscillator's local
coherence as a signal, which a phase network cannot expose. A Kuramoto
network read with an amplitude slaved to its own phases recovers about
half of the lead at gain 2 and little at gain 1, so the amplitude's
feedback on the phases carries the rest, and that part is not yet
explained. The per-feature class-information maps
(\hyperref[fig-fig11-appD2-class-information]{Figures 11} and
\hyperref[fig-fig12-appD2-class-information-seeds]{12}) do not track
this accuracy, and \hyperref[sec-D-2]{Appendix D.2} says why. Among
phase oscillators the coupling function and the geometry each moved
accuracy by less than a point, and a coil and a cochlea, built to follow
the cochlea's shape and mechanics, read no better than the torus.

\textbf{Readout.} With noise, more training data raised every arm, the
trained baselines three to six times as much as the Kuramoto network,
since in a reservoir only the readout learns. The common width of 192
favours the input: it reads the spectrogram's 192 features whole but
compresses a reservoir's 12,288 to 24,576 features by 64 to 128 times
through a random projection, which keeps the distances between clips
only roughly at so few dimensions \citep{johnson1984}. Read at 4,096,
the coupled network and the leaky-integrator banks at gain 1 pass their
input, but their readout then fits 40,970 weights against the 1,930 that
read the input, so some or all of the improvement could be the extra
weights. The fair comparison is at the common width.
\hyperref[sec-4-8]{Section 4.8} shows that the comparisons read the same
through a PCA fitted on the training clips, while a pooling that
averages each lattice row keeps the Stuart--Landau lead only at gain 2.
The conclusions therefore do not rest on one random matrix, though they
do rest on a reader that sees within a row.

\textbf{Data and training.} Many published spoken-digit results share
speakers between training and test, among them both noisy AudioMNIST
studies \citep{beoletto2026, young2026} and the TI-46 reservoirs
\citep{torrejon2017, opala2019, coulombe2017}. A classifier there, a
trained network or a readout alike, can learn the test speakers' voices,
which likely raises its accuracy, so those results are not comparable
with ours. Training is the step these results leave open, and the same
controls can say where it helps: a learned encoder, a learned decoder in
place of the random projection, or trained coupling and natural
frequencies, each set against the uncoupled network, the bank and the
input alone at the same number of trained parameters. A trained
coupled-oscillator network reaches 89\% on AudioMNIST from the raw
waveform \citep{chandravadia2026}, on a random split. Run through these
controls on held-out speakers, it would show what its oscillators
contribute.

\textbf{Future directions.} The input's format decided more than the
network's design, so other formats should be tested, such as MFCCs,
cochleagrams or a resonator's tonogram, on which a linear readout
reached about 98\% in hardware against 75.5\% for a spectrogram, with
shared speakers \citep{beoletto2026}. An untrained network also runs
causally, so with a streaming readout it could suit low-power, real-time
audio, which our clip-pooled readout leaves untested.

\textbf{Scope.} The conclusions are about one front end (a 16-band
log-mel spectrogram at 62.5 frames per second), one network size (1,024
oscillators in four 16 \ensuremath{\times} 16 channels), one readout
family (ridge on three time-pooled statistics per signal, at a common
width) and one task family (isolated spoken digits and their order, in
white noise, on held-out speakers). Within that scope they are
controlled: every comparison holds everything but one factor fixed.
Beyond it they are hypotheses. A richer front end could leave the
reservoir less to add or more. A larger lattice or a trained coupling
could change what coupling contributes. A streaming or nonlinear readout
could read structure these statistics discard, and other corpora, noises
or tasks could move the balance between input, memory and dynamics. The
standard reservoir baselines of \hyperref[sec-4-8]{Section 4.8} say how
the untrained oscillator network stands against the generic alternatives
at this size, which is what a trained oscillator network of the same
size should be measured against.

\textbf{Limitations.} Three seeds give a spread but not a precise
estimate of it. The readout is one structure, a ridge on three
statistics per signal. The trained baselines are matched at about 2,000
parameters and share one training recipe rather than one tuned to each
architecture, so each might read higher with a different training
regime.

\textbf{Conclusion.} Untrained and read at a common width, a coupled
oscillator network classifies noisy spoken digits from held-out speakers
about as well as its own input and carries the order of events by
integrating it, as a leaky-integrator bank does. Coupling adds to both
tasks, a free amplitude lifts the network above its input in noise, and
the lattice geometry and phase coupling function each move it by less
than a point. An echo state network of the same size is the strongest
untrained arm on clean audio and, level with the bank, on order, but it
reads just below the input in noise. Small trained networks read higher
with noise, the Stuart--Landau network alone reaching their range, and
these results are the baseline a trained oscillator network of similar
size should beat.

\section{Lattice geometries}\label{sec-C}

In our experiment, every channel stores its 256 oscillators as a 16
\ensuremath{\times} 16 grid whose row r is driven by mel band r. A
lattice geometry changes only which oscillators are neighbours, that is,
how the grid's edges are glued. Each of a network's four channels is a
separate copy of the same shape, and the kernel, the parameters and the
oscillators are the same under every geometry, so a comparison between
geometries varies one thing. The torus is the reference every other
geometry is compared against. Wrapped in both directions, it has no
edges, so every oscillator has the same neighbourhood and no position in
the lattice is special, and each other geometry departs from it in one
respect: opening edges, adding a dimension, or laying frequency out
differently. Here no geometry read above it by more than 0.21 points
(\hyperref[sec-4-4]{Section 4.4}).

\cmidrule{1-2}\textbf{Sheet} & Neither axis wraps, and coupling stops at

wrapped axes from zero to two. \\
\cmidrule{1-2}\textbf{Helix} & The 256 sites read as one closed ring,
one octave (64 sites, four rows) per turn, so an offset of one turn
joins bands an octave apart. \\
\cmidrule{1-2}\textbf{Cube} & Each row's 16 columns read as a 4

\ensuremath{\times} 4 lattice that wraps on all three axes: the same
oscillators, with shorter paths between them. \\
\cmidrule{1-2}\textbf{Sphere} & Rows as latitudes with open poles and
columns as longitudes that wrap, each oscillator's influence weighted by
the cosine of its latitude: an approximation to a sphere, not exact
spherical coupling. \\
\cmidrule{1-2}\textbf{Coil} & The 256 sites read as one open line from
the base (highest band) to the apex (lowest band), one octave per turn:
the helix with its ends apart, as the cochlea's spiral is open at both
ends, so an offset of one turn joins sites that sit side by side on
adjacent turns. \\
\cmidrule{1-2}\textbf{Cochlea} & The coil with two features of the
cochlea's mechanics: influence runs three times as strongly from base to
apex as back, as the travelling wave does, and the coupling into each
site grows with the coil's curvature, from a quarter at the base to full
strength at the apex, as the spiral tightens. A control keeps both at
the coil's average coupling. \\
\end{longtable}

The first six vary the number of wrapped axes (sheet, cylinder, torus),
the dimension (the cube, which shortens the paths between oscillators)
and how frequency is laid out: the cylinder and the sphere leave the
frequency axis open, as the cochlea does, and the helix puts bands an
octave apart next to each other, as the pitch helix of music perception
does \citep{shepard1982}. The helix is not a cochlea: it is closed, and
nothing in it runs one way. The coil and the cochlea follow the cochlea
itself, whose coiled, tonotopic shape a spiral metamaterial resonator
has used to classify AudioMNIST digits \citep{beoletto2026}. With
tonotopic natural frequencies, each coil position's frequency falls
exponentially from base to apex, as along the basilar membrane.
Oscillator lattices are a long-studied setting in physics
\citep{sakaguchi1987}, and machine-learning systems couple oscillators
all-to-all \citep{unconv-ai2026}, through attention, or locally on a
lattice \citep{keller2023, miyato2025}. Geometry has been varied in
several of the systems the survey covers, ``and in none of them as a
single variable'', and it found no work that varies coupling range or
boundary conditions at a matched budget \citep{kryski2026}.

\subsection{Where each arm carries class information}\label{sec-D-2}

Following \citet{beoletto2026}, a one-way analysis of variance measures
where in time and frequency each arm carries information about the
digit. Every signal of an arm is averaged over each sixteenth of the
padded clip (frames 0 to 61, warm-up included, so the input's onset
shows), and each average is tested for a difference between the ten
digits over the 2,048 training clips of seed 0 at 0 dB (gain 1 for the
reservoirs). F is the variance between digits over the variance within
them, each over its degrees of freedom. The maps average F over the
signals of each mel band: the band itself for the spectrogram, and the
sine and cosine of every oscillator, or every integrator's state, in
that band's row, over channels and columns. They describe where the
information sits. They do not compare accuracy.

The input carries its information in a burst at the onset: its F peaks
at 425 in band 4 in the fourth window, and averages 119 over windows 7
to 16. Every reservoir peaks later and lower (the Kuramoto network at
307, the Stuart--Landau network at 284 and the state-matched
leaky-integrator bank at 378, in the fifth or sixth window) and holds
more information afterwards, averaging 151 to 158 over windows 7 to 16,
and 165 to 172 in the lower eight bands, where the input averages 110
(\hyperref[fig-fig11-appD2-class-information]{Figure 11}).

Over all three seeds and both noise levels

band, and the Stuart--Landau network redistributes it: against the
Kuramoto network it carries less in mel bands 0 to 7 (6.9 F less at 0
dB, 4.2 at \ensuremath{-}5 dB) and more in bands 8 to 15 (15.8 F more at
0 dB, 8.0 at \ensuremath{-}5 dB, with a spread over seeds of 1.5 and
1.0). The Kuramoto network and the leaky-integrator bank add most of
theirs in the lower eight bands (Kuramoto: 179 against 139 mean F at 0
dB), and the Stuart--Landau network adds it more evenly (172 against
155). The input's own F in noise (133 against 129 in the upper and lower
eight bands at 0 dB) is not comparable with its F on clean audio and
does not mean its upper bands carry as much: the noise is scaled to each
clip's speech energy over the padded clip, so in noise the floor
stabilizes the quiet frames (the within-class variance of a window mean
falls ten to twenty times) and its level encodes the clip's loudness,
which differs by digit (the between-class variance stays at 0.23 in the
silent padding windows, where clean silence has none), and the padding
windows alone reach F of 120 at 0 dB against 1 clean. A readout on those
windows alone reads 17\% (chance 10\%), and adding them to the speech
windows changes nothing (80.9\% against 80.6\%), so the accuracies are
unaffected, though the per-feature maps in noise are not. What the
readout can get from each band is measured below by reading it alone.

points from gain 1 to 8 at 0 dB, and Kuramoto's does not, losing 17.4
(\hyperref[sec-4-5]{Section 4.5}). F measures how well each signal
separates the digits on its own, and the readout combines all 24,576 at
once. The band pattern is real and consistent over seeds, but it tracks
neither the Stuart--Landau network's lead across noise levels nor its
robustness to gain.

A readout restricted to one half of the lattice says where the readout's
gain from the amplitude sits. The row-readout experiment reads the

\ensuremath{\times} 3 statistics \ensuremath{\times} 4 windows = 96
features), at 0 and \ensuremath{-}5 dB, gains 1 and 2 and three seeds.
Through its lower eight rows alone the Stuart--Landau network reads 0.7
and \ensuremath{-}0.2 points against Kuramoto at gain 1 (3.2 and 3.4 at
gain 2). Through its upper eight alone it reads 4.4 and 5.5 points above
(7.8 and 9.3 at gain 2). So the amplitude's lead over the phase network
sits in the upper rows, where the Stuart--Landau network's signals also
carry more class information per feature, and the two measures agree on
where, if not on how much. Each network reads nearly as well through one

Stuart--Landau network and for Kuramoto's lower half), where the input's
upper eight bands alone read 41.6\% and 32.5\%, 30 to 35 points below

upper eight rows read like the input's upper bands (42.9\% and 31.6\%),
28 to 31 points below the coupled network's, while through the lower
eight rows the two networks read within a point of each other.

\textbf{What the readout gets from each band.} The band-readout
experiment reads each arm through one row of its lattice alone (the
within-row pooling kept for one row, 3 statistics \ensuremath{\times} 4
windows = 12 features), for every row, at gain 1 and three seeds
(\hyperref[fig-fig16-appD2-band-readout]{Figure 13}). On clean audio a
single band of the input reads 38\% to 58\%. The Kuramoto and uncoupled
networks read each band 5 to 10 points below the input on average, and
the Stuart--Landau network reads the lower eight bands at the input's
level (53\% against 51\%) and the upper eight below it (47\% against
53\%). In noise the picture changes. The input's upper eight bands
collapse to 25\% on average at 0 dB (22\% at \ensuremath{-}5 dB), and
the uncoupled network and the leaky-integrator bank follow them (25\%
and 24\%), since each row of theirs holds only its own band's history.
The Kuramoto network's upper rows read 44\% (38\% at \ensuremath{-}5
dB), the lower bands' information carried in by the coupling. The
Stuart--Landau network's upper rows read 51\% (45\% at \ensuremath{-}5
dB), more than its own lower rows (48\% and 43\%) and more than any
other arm's single band: the rows that receive only a noise-dominated
drive become the best single-band readouts in the study. In the lower
eight bands the Stuart--Landau network leads Kuramoto by 3 points at 0
dB and 2 at \ensuremath{-}5 dB, and in the upper eight by 8 and 7. So
the amplitude's gain sits where the drive is weak and noisy and the
coupling is the row's main input, which is what a reading of the
amplitude as a weight on each oscillator's local coherence predicts.
\hyperref[sec-5]{Section 5} states that reading and the experiments that
test it.

\textbf{What the amplitude gives the readout.} The amplitude-view
experiment reads the Stuart--Landau network three ways at the primary
cell (0 and \ensuremath{-}5 dB, gains 1 and 2, three seeds): through its
full signals r\ensuremath{\cdot}sin \ensuremath{\theta} and
r\ensuremath{\cdot}cos \ensuremath{\theta}, through its phase alone
(every oscillator at unit radius, z/\textbar z\textbar) and through its
amplitude alone (\textbar z\textbar, one signal per oscillator, so half
the features). Through its amplitude alone the network reads within a
point of its full read in every condition (80.0\% against 80.4\% at 0 dB
and gain 1; +0.3, \ensuremath{-}0.2, \ensuremath{-}0.2 and +1.2 points
over the four conditions) and keeps its whole lead over the Kuramoto
network (2.2 and 4.2 points at 0 dB, gains 1 and 2; 2.4 and 2.9 at
\ensuremath{-}5 dB, against 2.6, 4.0, 2.2 and 4.1 for the full read).
Through its phase alone it falls to within 0.7 and 2.4 points of
Kuramoto at 0 dB and 0.7 and 1.2 at \ensuremath{-}5 dB. So what the
readout gains from the amplitude is the amplitude itself as a signal, a
second state per oscillator whose windowed mean, spread and change carry
the class information, and not a weighting of the phase signals: the
radial equation makes r a record of the coupling's and the restoring
pull's radial force on an oscillator, which is how far it is out of step
with its neighbours and with phase 0, relaxed at the amplitude's own
rate. The same experiment reads the Kuramoto network with an amplitude
slaved to its phases (the Stuart--Landau radial equation driven by the
Kuramoto phases, with no feedback on them): that recovers 0.6 and 0.2
points of the lead at gain 1 and 2.7 and 2.1 at gain 2, about half of it
there, so the amplitude's feedback on the phases carries the rest. The
stiffness experiment asks whether the amplitude's slow relaxation
(\ensuremath{\alpha} = \ensuremath{\beta} = 0.05 per step, a time
constant of about 20 frames) is part of this: the exact-drive network
with its relaxation 4 and 16 times faster (time constants of about 5 and
1.25 frames) reads within 0.1 points of the rate-1 network at gain 1
(80.0\% and 79.8\% against 79.9\%), within 0.3 points at every gain at
rate \ensuremath{\times}4, and 1.0 and 1.6 points below it at gains 4
and 8 at rate \ensuremath{\times}16 (77.9\% and 75.8\% against 78.9\%
and 77.4\%), losing 4.2 points from gain 1 to 8 where the rate-1 network
loses 2.5 and Kuramoto 17.4. So the lead needs no slow amplitude memory,
and the slow relaxation adds only a little of the robustness to a strong
drive: the radius acts as a near-instantaneous signal of the radial
force on each oscillator.

\section{The discrete updates}\label{sec-E}

This appendix gives every reservoir's update as the code computes it,
with its solver, time step, initial state and random draws. Every
reservoir advances once per front-end frame, 16 ms, with a step of dt =
0.1 in the oscillators' units, so an oscillator at the mean natural
frequency \ensuremath{\omega} = 1 turns 0.1 rad per frame. Band r's
input at frame t is u\ensuremath{_{r}}(t) = max(0, (log
E\ensuremath{_{r}}(t) + 10)/10), where E\ensuremath{_{r}} is the band's
energy, which places silence near 0 and the loudest frames near 1.5. The
drive on every oscillator of row r, in every channel and column, is
d\ensuremath{_{i}}(t) = g\ensuremath{\cdot}u\ensuremath{_{r}}(t) at
input gain g. A run's seed s sets three draws: each channel's coupling
kernel, a 16 \ensuremath{\times} 16 table K \textasciitilde{} N(0,
0.05\ensuremath{^{2}}); its natural frequencies \ensuremath{\omega}
\textasciitilde{} N(1, 0.1\ensuremath{^{2}}) (random), or the tonotopic
or identical values of \hyperref[sec-4-4]{Section 4.4}; and every
oscillator's initial phase \ensuremath{\theta_{i}}(0) \textasciitilde{}
U{[}0, 2\ensuremath{\pi}), the same initial state for every clip of the
run. Each channel's kernel is then scaled so that the largest magnitude
of its two-dimensional Fourier transform is at most the coupling ceiling
c, by the single factor min(1, c/m), where m is that largest magnitude.
The coupling sums below run over every other oscillator of the channel,
computed as a circular convolution with K on the torus (the same
operator as a dense matrix on the other geometries). The
leaky-integrator bank and the reservoir baselines of
\hyperref[sec-G]{Appendix G} start from the zero state.

\textbf{Phase oscillators} (Kuramoto, Kuramoto--Sakaguchi, second
harmonic, Winfree). One explicit Euler step per frame,

\ensuremath{\theta_{i}} \ensuremath{\leftarrow} (\ensuremath{\theta_{i}}
+ dt\ensuremath{\cdot}(\ensuremath{\omega_{i}} + T\ensuremath{_{i}} +
d\ensuremath{_{i}} \ensuremath{-} \ensuremath{\lambda} sin
\ensuremath{\theta_{i}})) mod 2\ensuremath{\pi},

where T\ensuremath{_{i}} is the coupling term of
\hyperref[sec-B]{Appendix B} evaluated at the frame's start. The readout
sees sin \ensuremath{\theta_{i}} and cos \ensuremath{\theta_{i}}. The
drive advances the phase by exactly h\ensuremath{_{i}} =
dt\ensuremath{\cdot}d\ensuremath{_{i}} per frame, whatever the phase.

\textbf{Stuart--Landau oscillators.} The state is z\ensuremath{_{i}} =
x\ensuremath{_{i}} + i\ensuremath{\cdot}y\ensuremath{_{i}}. One frame is
three steps, an operator split. First, a semi-implicit Euler step for
the coupling, the restoring pull and (in the study's runs) the drive,

x\ensuremath{_{i}} \ensuremath{\leftarrow} x\ensuremath{_{i}} +
dt\ensuremath{\cdot}(\ensuremath{\Sigma_{j}} K\ensuremath{_{ij}}
(x\ensuremath{_{j}} \ensuremath{-} x\ensuremath{_{i}}) +
\ensuremath{\lambda}(1 \ensuremath{-} x\ensuremath{_{i}}) \ensuremath{-}
d\ensuremath{_{i}\cdot}y\ensuremath{_{i}}), y\ensuremath{_{i}}
\ensuremath{\leftarrow} y\ensuremath{_{i}} +
dt\ensuremath{\cdot}(\ensuremath{\Sigma_{j}} K\ensuremath{_{ij}}
(y\ensuremath{_{j}} \ensuremath{-} y\ensuremath{_{i}}) \ensuremath{-}
\ensuremath{\lambda}y\ensuremath{_{i}} +
d\ensuremath{_{i}\cdot}x\ensuremath{_{i}}),

the second line reading the x\ensuremath{_{i}} the first has just
updated. The coupling is the diffusive form of \hyperref[sec-B]{Appendix
B}, \ensuremath{\Sigma_{j}} K\ensuremath{_{ij}} (z\ensuremath{_{j}}
\ensuremath{-} z\ensuremath{_{i}}), and \ensuremath{\lambda}(1
\ensuremath{-} z) is a pull toward the point z = 1 whose tangential
component on the unit circle is \ensuremath{-\lambda} sin
\ensuremath{\theta}, the phase oscillators' restoring term. Second, the
amplitude step on r = \textbar z\textbar: the fixed-amplitude variant
sets z \ensuremath{\leftarrow} z/r, so \textbar z\textbar{} = 1 after
every frame. The free variant takes one backward-Euler step of dr/dt =
\ensuremath{\alpha}r \ensuremath{-} \ensuremath{\beta}r³ with
\ensuremath{\alpha} = \ensuremath{\beta} = 0.05, the single positive
root r' of dt\ensuremath{\cdot\beta\cdot}r'³ + (1 \ensuremath{-}
dt\ensuremath{\cdot\alpha})\ensuremath{\cdot}r' = r (Cardano's formula),
and sets z \ensuremath{\leftarrow} z\ensuremath{\cdot}r'/r, so the
amplitude relaxes toward radius 1 at rate \ensuremath{\alpha} and is
bounded by the cubic term, with no clamp. Third, an exact rotation by
the natural frequency, z \ensuremath{\leftarrow}
z\ensuremath{\cdot}exp(i\ensuremath{\cdot}dt\ensuremath{\cdot\omega_{i}}).
The readout sees Im z\ensuremath{_{i}} and Re z\ensuremath{_{i}}, which
for the free variant are r\ensuremath{_{i}} sin \ensuremath{\theta_{i}}
and r\ensuremath{_{i}} cos \ensuremath{\theta_{i}}. With
\textbar z\textbar{} held at 1 the tangential parts of the first step
are exactly the Kuramoto coupling and the restoring term, so the
fixed-amplitude variant is the phase model's equal-amplitude slice in
continuous time, and in discrete time matches the Kuramoto arm up to the
operator split's O(dt) error.

\textbf{The drive step.} The drive's part of the first step is the map
(x, y) \ensuremath{\to} (x \ensuremath{-} h\ensuremath{\cdot}y, y +
h\ensuremath{\cdot}(x \ensuremath{-} h\ensuremath{\cdot}y)), with h =
dt\ensuremath{\cdot}d.~Its matrix has trace 2 \ensuremath{-}
h\ensuremath{^{2}}, so for h \textless{} 2 it is conjugate to a rotation
by \ensuremath{\varphi}(h) = 2\ensuremath{\cdot}arcsin(h/2) rather than
h, and it does not preserve \textbar z\textbar, so after the amplitude
step a single frame's phase advance depends on the phase it started
from, while over many frames the advance averages
\ensuremath{\varphi}(h). The input is at most about 1.5, so h is at most
about 0.15\ensuremath{\cdot}g: at gain 1 the rotation is 0.1\% too far,
at gain 2 under 0.4\%, at gain 4 about 2\%, at gain 8 about 7\%, at gain
10 about 13\% and at gain 12 about 24\%, and the fixed-amplitude
variant's agreement with Kuramoto at gains 1 and 2
(\hyperref[sec-4-3]{Section 4.3}) is this error being negligible there.
At the sweep's high gains it is not, and it is the likely cause of the
fixed-amplitude variant's divergence from Kuramoto in
\hyperref[fig-fig06-sec4-5-restoring-ceiling-gain]{Figure 4}. The free
variant's drive step also stretches or shrinks \textbar z\textbar{}
before the amplitude relaxes, so part of what the amplitude absorbs at
high gain could be this discretization rather than the oscillator. To
test that, the exact-drive experiment (\hyperref[sec-4-5]{Section 4.5})
reruns both variants with the drive removed from the first step and
folded into the third as an exact rotation, z \ensuremath{\leftarrow}
z\ensuremath{\cdot}exp(i\ensuremath{\cdot}dt\ensuremath{\cdot}(\ensuremath{\omega_{i}}
+ d\ensuremath{_{i}})), which advances the phase by exactly h at any
gain, as the phase models do.

\textbf{Leaky-integrator bank.} x\ensuremath{_{i}}
\ensuremath{\leftarrow} (1 \ensuremath{-}
a\ensuremath{_{i}})\ensuremath{\cdot}x\ensuremath{_{i}} +
a\ensuremath{_{i}\cdot}tanh(g\ensuremath{_{i}\cdot}d\ensuremath{_{i}}),
with a\ensuremath{_{i}} = 1 \ensuremath{-}
exp(\ensuremath{-}1/(62.5\ensuremath{\cdot\tau_{i}})) for a time
constant \ensuremath{\tau_{i}} on a log-spaced schedule from 16 ms to 1
s and g\ensuremath{_{i}} \textasciitilde{} N(1, 0.1\ensuremath{^{2}}).

\section{The reference configuration, its calibration and model
selection}\label{sec-F}

\textbf{How the reference was fixed.} The reference configuration is
Kuramoto coupling on the torus with random natural frequencies,
restoring strength 0.3, coupling ceiling 1 and input gains 1 and 2.
Kuramoto is the reference coupling because it is the most widely used in
the prior art, which gives the most points of comparison. The torus is
the reference geometry because it wraps in both directions. Every
oscillator then has the same neighbourhood and no position in the
lattice is special, so the comparisons of coupling function, gain,
restoring strength and ceiling carry no boundary effect. Each other
geometry departs from it in one respect (\hyperref[sec-C]{Appendix C}),
and the coupling ceiling, defined through the kernel's spectrum, is
exact on it, since the coupling there is a circular convolution. Random
natural frequencies are the choice with no built-in structure. The two
noise levels are those at which \citet{young2026} trained. The
configuration was fixed in advance, and the calibration below checks it
on validation speakers that are never in the test set. The ridge penalty
is chosen from four values on the last eighth of the training clips (on
the validation speakers where they are held out), then refit on all of
them.

\textbf{Calibration on the validation speakers.} Calibration and
selection hold the last eight training speakers out as a validation set:
speakers 1 to 40 train (20,000 clips), 41 to 48 validate (4,000) and 49
to 60 test (6,000), the same test set as every other experiment (the
code and records label these runs protocol V). The ridge then chooses
its penalty on the validation speakers, and everything below is read on
those speakers only. The calibration has two parts. The noise gate runs
the spectrogram-only baseline and the Kuramoto and Stuart--Landau
networks at the reference configuration, gains 1 and 2, on clean audio
and at noise 10 dB and 5 dB below the speech, as loud as it, and 5 dB
and 10 dB above it (the first table below). The study's two levels are
among those at which the strongest untrained network lands between 70
and 90\%. On the validation speakers the window admits noise from 10 dB
below the speech to 5 dB above it, with the study's two levels inside
it, and excludes clean audio, where the Stuart--Landau network reads
92.1\% (the Kuramoto network 87.3\%), and noise 10 dB above the speech,
where the strongest network reads 67.3\%. The configuration grid is the
selection experiment: for the Kuramoto network, gain \{0.25, 0.5, 1, 2\}
\ensuremath{\times} restoring strength \{0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 0.8, 1\}
\ensuremath{\times} coupling ceiling \{0.5, 1, 1.5, 2\} at the reference
geometry and frequencies, 80 configurations. For the Stuart--Landau
network it is gain \{0.5, 1, 2\} \ensuremath{\times} the same, 60
configurations. Every cell runs at 0 and \ensuremath{-}5 dB and three
seeds. The grid spans every restoring strength and ceiling the design
and sweep experiments ran, so the reference sits inside it on each axis.
The three tables after it read the grid one axis at a time, the other
two factors at their reference values, and
\hyperref[fig-fig14-appF-calibration]{Figure 15} shows the whole grid.
Along gain the Kuramoto network's validation accuracy falls by 2.5 to 3
points from 0.25 to 2 (81.9\% to 79.0\% at 0 dB, 75.4\% to 72.9\% at
\ensuremath{-}5 dB), so its reference gain of 1 sits 1 to 1.5 points
below the best value, while the Stuart--Landau network is flat within
0.6 points. Along restoring strength the Kuramoto network is flat within
a point and rises toward 1 (80.6\% to 81.6\% at 0 dB), while the
Stuart--Landau network is best at 0.1 to 0.3 and loses 3.4 and 3.7
points by 1, so the reference 0.3 is on its best plateau. Along the
coupling ceiling both networks are flat within two points and the
reference 1 is the best value or within 0.1 points of it. The reference
therefore agrees with the calibration on the noise levels, the restoring
strength and the ceiling, and its gain of 1 sits 1 to 1.5 points below
the Kuramoto network's best.

\section{The reservoir baselines}\label{sec-G}

Three reservoir baselines complete the comparison, all untrained, read
through the same statistics, projection and ridge as every other arm,
and sized to the oscillator network's 1,024 states.

\textbf{Echo state network.} The leaky echo state network
\citep{jaeger2001, jaeger2007}: 1,024 units with x
\ensuremath{\leftarrow} (1 \ensuremath{-} a)\ensuremath{\cdot}x +
a\ensuremath{\cdot}tanh(W x + V\ensuremath{\cdot}(g\ensuremath{\cdot}u)
+ b), where W is a sparse random matrix (about ten incoming connections
per unit, Gaussian weights) rescaled to a spectral radius
\ensuremath{\rho}, V and b are uniform on {[}\ensuremath{-}1, 1{]}, a is
the leaking rate and the input scaling is the run's gain g. Unlike every
other reservoir here, its input matrix is dense: every unit hears every
band, where the oscillator network and the bank route band r to row r.
Its hyperparameters were selected on the validation speakers
(\hyperref[sec-F]{Appendix F}) over \ensuremath{\rho} \ensuremath{\in}
\{0.5, 0.9, 1.1, 1.3\} (values above 1 included, since a spectral radius
below 1 is neither necessary nor sufficient for the echo state property
in a driven network, \citet{yildiz2012}), a \ensuremath{\in} \{0.1, 0.3,
1\} and g \ensuremath{\in} \{0.5, 1, 2\}, 36 configurations, following
the practice of \citet{lukosevicius2012}. The leaky-integrator bank of
\hyperref[sec-3-1]{Section 3.1} is this network with W = 0 and
row-routed input: what the echo state network adds is the random
recurrent mixing.

\textbf{Random oscillators network.} The RON of \citet{ceni2024}, a
reservoir of N damped harmonic oscillators forced through a random
layer: z\ensuremath{_{i}} \ensuremath{\leftarrow} z\ensuremath{_{i}} +
\ensuremath{\tau\cdot}(tanh(W y +
V\ensuremath{\cdot}(g\ensuremath{\cdot}u) + b)\ensuremath{_{i}}
\ensuremath{-} \ensuremath{\gamma_{i}} y\ensuremath{_{i}} \ensuremath{-}
\ensuremath{\epsilon_{i}} z\ensuremath{_{i}}), y\ensuremath{_{i}}
\ensuremath{\leftarrow} y\ensuremath{_{i}} +
\ensuremath{\tau\cdot}z\ensuremath{_{i}}, with each oscillator's
stiffness \ensuremath{\gamma_{i}} and damping \ensuremath{\epsilon_{i}}
drawn from uniform ranges, W uniform on {[}\ensuremath{-}2, 2{]}
rescaled to spectral radius \ensuremath{\rho}, V uniform on {[}0, 1{]}
and b uniform on {[}\ensuremath{-}1, 1{]}, as in their code. With N =
512 oscillators the network has 1,024 states, and the readout sees both
the positions y and the velocities z, as it sees the oscillator
network's sines and cosines. With \ensuremath{\epsilon} =
1/\ensuremath{\tau} and \ensuremath{\gamma} = 1 it reduces to the leaky
echo state network. Its hyperparameters were selected over
\ensuremath{\tau} \ensuremath{\in} \{0.1, 0.3\}, \ensuremath{\rho}
\ensuremath{\in} \{1, 3, 9\}, \ensuremath{\gamma} \ensuremath{\in} \{1
\ensuremath{\pm} 0.5, 5 \ensuremath{\pm} 4\}, \ensuremath{\epsilon}
\ensuremath{\in} \{0.5 \ensuremath{\pm} 0.4, 2 \ensuremath{\pm} 1.5\}
and g \ensuremath{\in} \{0.5, 1, 2\}, 72 configurations, within the
paper's guideline region (every \ensuremath{\epsilon} at most
2/\ensuremath{\tau} and every \ensuremath{\gamma} at most
2/\ensuremath{\tau^{2}}).

\textbf{Random nonlinear expansion.} The control for dimensionality
alone: h(t) = tanh(V\ensuremath{\cdot}(g\ensuremath{\cdot}u(t)) + b), a
random feature map of the current frame with 1,024 units (and 2,048, the
network's signal count), no recurrence and no memory, which is the echo
state network at \ensuremath{\rho} = 0 and a = 1
\citep{rahimi2007, huang2006}. It separates what a wider nonlinear view
of the same frame gives the readout from what dynamics give it, and on
the order task it must read chance, as the input alone does.

\textbf{Selected hyperparameters and results.} The validation speakers
selected, for the echo state network, spectral radius 1.3, leaking rate
0.1 and input scale 0.5 at both noise levels (validation accuracy 79.6\%
and 73.9\%), the largest spectral radius and the slowest leak in the
grid. For the random oscillators network they selected \ensuremath{\tau}
= 0.1, spectral radius 9, stiffness 1 \ensuremath{\pm} 0.5 and input
scale 0.5 at both levels, with damping 2 \ensuremath{\pm} 1.5 at 0 dB
and 0.5 \ensuremath{\pm} 0.4 at \ensuremath{-}5 dB (80.7\% and 75.5\%),
again the largest spectral radius offered. The table below gives their
test accuracy under Protocol A beside the network, its controls and the
expansion. Each selected reservoir runs on clean audio at its 0 dB
selection.
